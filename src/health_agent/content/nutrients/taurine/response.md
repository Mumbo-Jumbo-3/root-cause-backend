## What it's for

Taurine is a conditionally essential sulfonic acid — not a classical amino acid — that functions as a free molecule throughout the body rather than being incorporated into proteins. It is found in high concentrations in the brain, heart, skeletal muscle, retina, and immune cells, where it performs a wide range of roles.

**Neurological:** Taurine acts as a GABA agonist, promoting calm, focused mental states. It supports mood regulation by boosting dopamine while helping moderate excess serotonin, and has been clinically explored for neuroprotection in Alzheimer's disease and epilepsy.

**Cardiovascular:** It supports heart muscle function and rhythm, helps regulate blood pressure through sodium/potassium/calcium dynamics in cardiac cells, and reduces triglycerides.

**Liver and metabolic health:** Taurine supports bile acid synthesis and bile flow, can help reverse fatty liver, and modulates insulin signaling via IRS1 and GLUT4, making it relevant for blood sugar regulation and metabolic syndrome.

**Gut health:** It is strongly anti-inflammatory in the gut, supports a healthy microbiome, and is notably low in people with IBD.

**Antioxidant and mitochondrial function:** Taurine neutralizes reactive oxygen species, combats oxidative stress in retinal and adipose tissue, and supports mitochondrial health and energy metabolism.

**Electrolyte and mineral regulation:** It regulates intracellular electrolytes, plays a role in copper absorption, and is synergistic with magnesium for overall electrolyte balance.

**Hormonal and reproductive health:** Taurine supports androgen optimization and reduces oxidative stress affecting fertility, though high doses may influence prolactin levels.

Those who benefit most include vegans and vegetarians (who have no significant dietary source), people with IBD, fatty liver, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular concerns, or cognitive decline, as well as athletes seeking performance and recovery support.

## How to get it

**From food:** Taurine is found almost exclusively in animal-based foods. The richest sources are scallops and octopus (very high), beef heart and shellfish broadly (high), other seafood and red meats (moderate to high), and seaweed (moderate). Vegans and vegetarians obtain very little taurine from diet and are most likely to benefit from supplementation.

**Supplement forms:**
- **Standard L-Taurine powder or capsules** — the most common, well-studied, and cost-effective form, suitable for general health, cardiovascular, metabolic, and gut support.
- **N-Acetyltaurine (acetyltaurate)** — more brain-directed and more strongly GABAergic; preferred when the primary goal is cognitive or mood support.

Pure powder is generally preferable to capsules for purity reasons, as capsule products often include flow agents or capsule-shell excipients.

**Practical combinations:**
- **Coffee + Taurine + L-Theanine:** A popular calm-focus stack; taurine's GABAergic properties buffer caffeine's anxiety-inducing effects.
- **Taurine + Magnesium:** Synergistic for electrolyte balance and heart health.
- **Taurine + Glycine + Choline:** Supports liver, bile flow, and gut health.

**Timing:** Morning is generally preferred, as taurine can produce a mild energy-boosting effect that may interfere with sleep if taken late in the day.

## Dosing

Start with dietary sources as your foundation. For those who eat animal foods regularly, meaningful amounts of taurine are obtained from diet. Supplementation becomes most relevant for vegans, vegetarians, or those with elevated therapeutic needs.

As a supplement, a practical framework is:

- **General health and maintenance:** 500 mg–1,000 mg per day — appropriate as a baseline daily intake for most people supplementing for general support.
- **Active therapeutic use** (metabolic health, cardiovascular support, gut inflammation, athletic recovery): 1,000 mg–3,000 mg per day, split across two or more doses.
- **Performance and longevity protocols:** 3,000 mg–6,000 mg per day in split doses, used by those with specific goals; this range warrants more attention to the risks described below.
- **Suggested upper limit:** 6,000–7,000 mg per day, beyond which some practitioners raise concern about prolactin elevation and other effects.

**If combining taurine with L-carnitine or CoQ10,** reduce your taurine dose below the standard 500–1,000 mg range, as the combination significantly affects absorption kinetics and can become excessive systemically.

## Risks

**Side effects and upper limits:** Taurine has a strong safety profile at reasonable doses. Long-term supplementation is considered safe when kidney and liver function are normal and other amino acids are reasonably balanced. At doses above 6–7 g per day, some practitioners raise concern about elevated prolactin, which may be particularly relevant for men focused on hormonal optimization. Taurine does not have a strong case for mandatory cycling.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **Antihypertensive medications:** Taurine independently lowers blood pressure; combining it with blood pressure medications may cause excessive drops. Consult your doctor before combining.
- **L-Carnitine and CoQ10:** When used together, reduce taurine dose below the standard range to avoid excessive systemic accumulation.
- **Magnesium:** Synergistic and generally safe to combine.
- **Antibiotics and other medications:** As with any supplement, disclose taurine use to your physician.
- **Breastfeeding:** Caution is advised due to the hormonal and physiological demands of lactation.
- **Kidney or liver disease:** Those with compromised kidney or liver function should consult a clinician before supplementing.
