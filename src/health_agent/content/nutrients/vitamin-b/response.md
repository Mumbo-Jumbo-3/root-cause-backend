## What it's for

The B vitamin complex comprises eight essential water-soluble vitamins that serve as cofactors in hundreds of biochemical reactions throughout your body. These vitamins are crucial for energy metabolism by converting carbohydrates, fats, and proteins into usable energy. They support nervous system function and nerve transmission, produce neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine for mood regulation, and create healthy red blood cells that carry oxygen. B vitamins also support methylation and detoxification processes for cellular repair and toxin elimination, while maintaining robust immune function. Specific B vitamins have specialized roles - B1 (thiamine) is essential for carbohydrate metabolism and nerve function, B6 supports amino acid metabolism and neurotransmitter production, B12 is critical for nerve health and DNA synthesis, and folate is vital for cell division and pregnancy health.

## How to get it

The most bioavailable forms of B vitamins come from animal products and fermented foods. Top-tier sources include organ meats (especially liver) as the most concentrated source, grass-fed red meat, raw dairy and kefir, egg yolks for choline and biotin, and fish and poultry for B6 and B12. Secondary sources include fermented foods like fermented vegetables and nutritional yeast, spirulina and brewer's yeast, mushrooms for B2 and B3 content, and sprouted grains cooked in bone broth. For most people, prioritizing these whole food sources will provide adequate B vitamins in their most bioavailable forms. Supplementation should be considered as a short-term intervention rather than a permanent solution, with preference given to food-based sources when additional B vitamins are needed.

## Dosing

For dietary intake, focus on incorporating B vitamin-rich foods daily through organ meats 1-2 times weekly, quality animal products at each meal, and fermented foods regularly. When supplementation is needed - particularly for vegans, those with metabolic disorders, people consuming excess carbohydrates/alcohol, or those with MTHFR genetic variations - take B-complex supplements in activated forms during the morning as B vitamins can be stimulating. Use bioactive forms like P5P (pyridoxal 5'-phosphate) for B6 instead of pyridoxine HCl, and methylcobalamin or hydroxocobalamin for B12 rather than cyanocobalamin. Cycle supplementation for 2-8 weeks on, then take breaks, and limit B6 as P5P to 2-week cycles. Take supplements with meals containing the macronutrients they help metabolize, and avoid megadoses unless medically supervised.

## Risks

Avoid combining B vitamins with vitamin C, H2 medications, or large amounts of caffeine in the same meal. Niacin shouldn't be taken with blood thinners, aspirin, or ginkgo. B6 in pyridoxine HCl form can cause peripheral neuropathy, especially at doses above 50mg, which is why the P5P form is preferred. Signs of deficiency include chronic fatigue, memory issues, mood disorders, poor wound healing, and neurological symptoms. Thiamine and niacin deficiencies are particularly associated with increased viral infection susceptibility and metabolic dysfunction. Take B vitamins earlier in the day as they can be stimulating and interfere with sleep when taken late.
