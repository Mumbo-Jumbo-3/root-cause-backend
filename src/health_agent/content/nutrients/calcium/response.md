## What it's for

Calcium is the most abundant mineral in the human body, with approximately 99% stored in bones and teeth to provide structural integrity. Beyond skeletal health, it plays essential roles throughout the body:

- **Bone and teeth formation and maintenance** — the structural foundation of the skeleton throughout life
- **Muscle contraction** — including the heart; calcium triggers the mechanical process of contraction and is required for regular cardiac rhythm
- **Nerve signaling** — calcium ions carry impulses between nerve cells
- **Hormonal regulation** — inadequate calcium causes parathyroid hormone (PTH) to rise chronically, pulling calcium out of bones and depositing it in soft tissues, arteries, and organs — a process associated with bone loss, cardiovascular risk, and systemic physiological stress
- **Reproductive and androgen health** — calcium stimulates luteinizing hormone (LH) production
- **Blood pressure regulation** — low calcium intake is associated with hypertension; adequate bioavailable calcium helps suppress PTH and support normal vascular function

## How to get it

**Food first.** Whole-food sources are consistently preferred over isolated supplements because they provide calcium alongside natural co-factors that improve absorption and utilization.

**Top food sources:**
- **Raw or minimally processed dairy** (goat preferred) — highly bioavailable, contains phosphorus and other co-factors
- **Bone broth and bone marrow** — calcium in a whole-food matrix with collagen and supporting minerals
- **Eggshell powder** — surprisingly bioavailable; can be homemade or purchased
- **Mineral water** — a useful daily contributor with no calories
- **Leafy greens, seafood, shellfish** — secondary sources; note that high-oxalate greens like spinach have sharply reduced calcium bioavailability due to oxalate binding

**Critical co-factors — calcium cannot work properly without them:**
- **Vitamin D** — dramatically increases intestinal calcium absorption; without it, dietary calcium is poorly absorbed regardless of intake
- **Vitamin K2** — directs calcium into bones and teeth rather than soft tissues and arteries; essential when increasing calcium intake, especially via supplements
- **Magnesium** — works synergistically with calcium for muscle and nerve function; aim for robust daily intake alongside calcium
- **Boron** — supports calcium and magnesium metabolism; found in prunes, raisins, and avocados

**Maintain a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of ≥1:1.** Excess phosphorus from processed foods and sodas worsens bone resorption.

**Reduce antinutrient interference** by soaking, fermenting, or sprouting grains and legumes to lower phytate content, which otherwise binds calcium and blocks absorption.

**Supplement forms, when needed:**
- **Calcium from whole-food concentrates** (eggshell calcium, bone meal) are preferred over isolated salts
- Among salts, **calcium citrate** absorbs well without food or high stomach acid; **calcium carbonate** requires stomach acid and should be taken with meals
- Never supplement calcium without also ensuring adequate vitamin K2 and vitamin D — this is the primary scenario where arterial calcification risk becomes relevant

## Dosing

The general adult target is **1,000–1,200 mg of elemental calcium per day from all sources combined** (food plus supplements). Most adults consuming dairy, mineral water, and varied whole foods can meet a meaningful portion of this through diet.

Use supplements to fill gaps, not as a primary strategy. If supplementing, **split doses** — for example, 500 mg twice daily rather than one large dose — because absorption efficiency drops with larger single amounts. Demand is higher during pregnancy, lactation, adolescence, post-menopause, and older age, and supplementation is more likely to be warranted in those contexts.

Always pair supplemental calcium with vitamin D and K2. Without them, higher calcium intakes carry greater risk of soft-tissue deposition rather than bone incorporation.

## Risks

**Deficiency risks:**
- Bone loss and osteoporosis over time
- Chronically elevated PTH, driving soft-tissue and arterial calcification, stress hormone dysregulation, and cardiovascular dysfunction
- Hypertension
- Counterintuitively, **low** dietary calcium increases kidney stone risk by allowing oxalate to be absorbed through the gut; adequate dietary calcium binds oxalate in the intestine before it reaches the kidneys

**Excess and toxicity risks:**
- **Hypercalcemia** from excessive supplementation: fatigue, confusion, kidney stones, cardiac arrhythmias
- **Arterial calcification**: primarily a risk when calcium supplements are taken without K2 and vitamin D, or in high isolated doses — calcium is not properly directed to bone
- The tolerable upper intake level for adults is generally cited at **2,000–2,500 mg/day** from all sources; staying well below this threshold is advisable

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **Vitamin D** — synergistic; required for adequate absorption; always combine
- **Vitamin K2** — prevents soft-tissue deposition; essential when supplementing
- **Magnesium** — competes with calcium for absorption in large simultaneous doses; balance and timing matter
- **Iron supplements** — calcium reduces iron absorption when taken together; separate by at least two hours
- **Fluoroquinolone and tetracycline antibiotics** — calcium chelates these drugs, reducing their absorption; take calcium well away from antibiotic doses
- **Alcohol** — impairs calcium absorption and increases urinary calcium loss
- **High caffeine intake** — may interfere with muscle calcium handling
- **Phytic acid and oxalic acid** — reduce absorption from specific foods; minimize through proper food preparation
- **Heavy metals (lead, cadmium)** — compete with calcium at absorption channels; broad mineral adequacy is protective
