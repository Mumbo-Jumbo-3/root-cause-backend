## What it's for

Iodine is an essential trace mineral the body cannot produce on its own, making dietary intake or supplementation necessary. Its most critical role is **thyroid hormone synthesis** — it is the core structural component of both T4 (thyroxine) and T3 (triiodothyronine), hormones that regulate metabolism, body temperature, heart rate, and energy production. Beyond the thyroid, iodine is indispensable for:

- **Brain development and neurological function**: Iodine deficiency during fetal development and infancy is one of the world's leading preventable causes of intellectual disability; even moderate deficiency correlates with measurable decreases in IQ and cognitive performance.
- **Growth and reproductive health**: Iodine supports endometrial health, Leydig cell function (involved in testosterone production), and overall hormonal balance.
- **Immune defense**: Iodine is required for antimicrobial peptide production and has direct antiviral and antibacterial properties.
- **Detoxification**: Adequate iodine helps displace toxic halogens such as fluoride and chlorine, which compete with iodine at thyroid receptors, and supports removal of certain heavy metals.

Both deficiency *and* excess cause real harm. The goal is sufficiency — not aggressive supplementation.

## How to get it

**Whole food sources are strongly preferred** over supplements. Marine foods offer the highest bioavailability:

- **Oysters** — an excellent source, with the added benefit of synergistic nutrients like zinc, selenium, and copper
- **Shrimp and other shellfish**
- **Cod and other fin fish**
- **Seaweed/kelp** — among the richest sources but with highly variable iodine content by species and harvest location; use intermittently (roughly once a week at most) rather than daily, due to the risk of excessive intake
- **Dairy products**, **eggs**, and **beef liver** — moderate sources; liver also supplies vitamins A and B12, which are critical for thyroid hormone utilization
- **Iodized table salt or sea salt** — a reliable baseline source, particularly in iodine-deficient regions

**Iodine requires co-factors to function properly.** Before considering supplementation, ensure adequate intake of selenium (essential for converting T4 to active T3 and protective against thyroid inflammation), vitamin A, B vitamins (especially riboflavin/B2), vitamin B12, vitamin C, zinc, iron, and vitamin D. Beef liver and seafood together cover many of these simultaneously.

**What blocks iodine:** Raw goitrogenic foods — soy, cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, Brussels sprouts), and cassava — can inhibit iodine uptake by the thyroid. Cooking significantly reduces this effect, and moderate consumption of cooked cruciferous vegetables is generally fine. Environmental halogens including fluoride (tap water, toothpaste), chlorine (tap water), and bromine (some breads, medications) compete with iodine at thyroid receptors and should be minimized where practical.

**If supplementing**, liquid drops or simple capsule formats with minimal excipients are preferable. Potassium iodide and nascent (atomic) iodine are the most bioavailable supplemental forms. Lugol's solution (elemental iodine + potassium iodide) is a time-tested option providing both forms simultaneously. Kelp capsules, if used, should contain no more than 250 µg per serving.

## Dosing

The baseline daily requirement for most adults is **150 µg/day**, with a functional target range of **150–300 µg/day** for general health. Requirements increase during pregnancy (220–250 µg/day) and breastfeeding (250–290 µg/day); food-based sources and prenatal vitamins containing 150–200 µg are the safest approaches for these groups.

For most people eating seafood 2–3 times per week, using iodized or sea salt daily, and consuming dairy and eggs regularly, the RDA is readily met through diet alone without any supplementation.

**If supplementing** — for example, when food sources are consistently inadequate or during a defined repletion period — keep doses to **no more than 150–300 µg per serving**, taken in the morning. Supplementing 1–3 times per week rather than daily is often sufficient and reduces the risk of excess. Avoid exceeding the tolerable upper limit of **1,100 µg/day** from all sources combined. Iodine supplementation should be cycled (a few weeks at most) rather than taken indefinitely, and only after ensuring selenium and other co-factors are adequate. Always consult a clinician before supplementing if you have any thyroid condition or take any medications.

## Risks

**Side effects and upper limits:** Excess iodine (above ~1,100 µg/day) can suppress thyroid function through the Wolff-Chaikoff effect — paradoxically causing or worsening hypothyroidism. In people with high stored polyunsaturated fats in tissues, rapid iodine loading can mobilize halogenated fats, causing skin rashes and detox-like reactions. Hyperthyroidism is also possible with chronic excess intake. Symptoms of iodine toxicity include a burning sensation in the mouth, throat, and stomach, nausea, vomiting, and in severe cases, thyroid dysfunction.

**Autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto's):** High iodine intake can significantly worsen Hashimoto's thyroiditis. Anyone with confirmed autoimmune thyroid disease should avoid iodine supplementation without direct medical supervision; selenium has stronger evidence for supporting Hashimoto's.

**Hyperthyroidism / Graves' disease:** Iodine should generally be minimized; consult an endocrinologist before making any changes to intake.

**Hypothyroidism:** Excess iodine can *suppress* rather than support thyroid function. Addressing co-factor deficiencies (selenium, vitamins A, D, B12) should take priority over iodine loading.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:** Iodine has clinically significant interactions with several medications and nutrients:
- **Thyroid medications** (levothyroxine, methimazole, propylthiouracil) — iodine can alter their effectiveness
- **Lithium** — both lithium and iodine independently affect thyroid function; combined use increases risk of thyroid dysregulation
- **Amiodarone** — this cardiac medication contains large amounts of iodine itself and can cause thyroid dysfunction
- **Certain diuretics and antihypertensives** — interactions have been noted; disclose iodine use to prescribers
- **Selenium and iodine** — selenium must be adequate before increasing iodine; iodine without sufficient selenium can exacerbate thyroid inflammation
- **Goitrogenic foods** — not a drug interaction, but high intake of raw goitrogens combined with borderline iodine intake can compound deficiency risk

Always disclose iodine supplementation to your prescribing physician, especially if you are on any thyroid, cardiac, or psychiatric medication.
