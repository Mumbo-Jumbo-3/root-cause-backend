## What it's for

Selenium is an essential trace mineral — the body cannot produce it and must obtain it through diet. It is a structural component of at least 25 selenoproteins that perform critical roles across multiple body systems.

**Thyroid hormone metabolism** is arguably selenium's most critical function. It is the cofactor for deiodinase enzymes that convert inactive T4 into the active thyroid hormone T3. The thyroid gland holds the body's highest concentration of selenium for this reason. Deficiency impairs this conversion, affecting metabolism, energy, mood, and body composition.

**Antioxidant defense** is selenium's other major role. It is central to the glutathione peroxidase enzyme system, which neutralizes free radicals, helps regenerate vitamins C and E, and protects cell membranes and DNA from oxidative damage.

Beyond these two pillars, selenium supports **heavy metal detoxification** by binding mercury and other metals into inert complexes for elimination — one reason seafood containing both mercury and selenium is less dangerous than mercury exposure without dietary selenium. Selenium also modulates **immune function**, with deficiency linked to increased infection susceptibility and autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's); clinical research has shown 200 µg/day of selenomethionine significantly reduces thyroid peroxidase antibodies in Hashimoto's patients. Additional roles include **DNA synthesis and repair**, **male reproductive health and sperm quality**, and **cardiovascular protection** through prevention of oxidative damage to cholesterol and polyunsaturated fats.

## How to get it

**Food is the preferred source** for the vast majority of people. A small daily serving of any of the following typically meets selenium needs:

- **Brazil nuts** — among the highest food sources; 1–3 nuts per day is generally sufficient; do not regularly exceed 5/day as individual nuts can contain 70–90 µg each
- **Shellfish** (oysters, clams, shrimp) — excellent source that also provides complementary zinc, copper, and iodine
- **Wild-caught fin fish** (salmon, tuna, sardines, cod) — high selenium; wild-caught preferred
- **Beef and organ meats** (especially liver) — reliable selenium with complementary minerals
- **Eggs** (free-range) — selenium plus sulfur amino acids that support glutathione
- **Turkey and chicken** — good everyday sources
- **Dairy** — moderate contributor

**Supplementation** may be warranted for confirmed deficiency, residence in selenium-depleted soil regions (parts of Europe, New Zealand, some areas of China and Africa), active Hashimoto's thyroiditis, poor dietary diversity, or significant heavy metal exposure.

When supplementing, **L-selenomethionine** is the preferred form. It is an organic, amino acid-bound form with approximately 85–90% absorption — superior to inorganic forms such as selenite or selenate — and closely mimics how selenium appears in food proteins. An alternative whole-food-derived option provides multiple naturally occurring selenium compounds (L-selenomethionine, Se-methyl-L-selenocysteine, and related forms) from selenium-enriched plant sources, which some consider the most food-like supplemental option.

Selenium works synergistically with **vitamin C** (essential for selenium metabolism), **vitamin E** (mutual antioxidant regeneration), **zinc** (hormone balance and immune regulation), and **NAC** (which provides cysteine for glutathione synthesis, complementing selenium's activation of glutathione peroxidase). Avoid high-dose iodine supplementation without first ensuring adequate selenium status, particularly with thyroid conditions — iodine can stress selenium-dependent thyroid enzymes when selenium is insufficient.

## Dosing

The baseline dietary target is **55–200 µg per day** from food, which most people can meet through a small daily serving of selenium-rich foods as listed above.

When supplementation is added — due to deficiency, regional soil depletion, or a clinical condition such as Hashimoto's — a typical supplemental dose is **100–200 µg/day of L-selenomethionine**, taken in addition to whatever is obtained from diet. Clinical studies on Hashimoto's have specifically used **200 µg/day**. Total intake from all sources (food plus supplement) should remain well under the tolerable upper limit of **400 µg/day**. Do not exceed 200–400 µg/day total without medical supervision, and get bloodwork (serum selenium or selenoprotein P) if you suspect deficiency or are managing a thyroid condition.

## Risks

**Toxicity (selenosis)** is the primary concern, as selenium has a narrow therapeutic window. Chronic intake above 400 µg/day can cause garlic-like breath odor (from dimethyl selenide), hair loss, brittle nails, nausea, diarrhea, fatigue, skin lesions, and neurological symptoms in severe cases. Those at highest risk include people eating large quantities of Brazil nuts daily and those layering high-dose supplements on top of a selenium-rich diet.

**Deficiency** carries its own serious risks: Keshan disease (cardiomyopathy) and Kashin-Beck disease (bone and joint disorder) occur in severely deficient populations. Deficiency is also associated with impaired T4→T3 conversion, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, weakened immune function, elevated oxidative stress, and impaired male fertility.

**Drug and nutrient interactions to be aware of:**

- **Mercury and heavy metals** directly compete with and deplete selenium; high-mercury fish and environmental mercury exposure are therefore particularly harmful when selenium status is low
- **High-dose iodine supplementation** without adequate selenium can worsen thyroid stress by overwhelming selenium-dependent enzymes
- **Vitamin C deficiency** impairs selenium metabolism directly, reducing its utilization
- **Chemotherapy agents** — selenium's antioxidant activity may theoretically interfere with some oxidative chemotherapy mechanisms; consult an oncologist before supplementing during cancer treatment
- **Anticoagulants** — some evidence suggests high-dose selenium may affect platelet function; those on blood thinners should consult a clinician
- **Poorly formulated selenium supplements** using inorganic forms at high doses carry higher toxicity risk; choose clean L-selenomethionine from reputable, third-party-tested manufacturers
