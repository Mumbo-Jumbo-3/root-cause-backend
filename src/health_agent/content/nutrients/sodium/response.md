## What it's for

Sodium is one of the body's primary electrolytes — a charged mineral dissolved in blood and extracellular fluid that is essential for survival. Its roles extend far beyond the oversimplified narrative that it merely raises blood pressure.

**Nerve and muscle function.** Sodium drives nerve impulse conduction and is required for muscles to contract and relax. Without it, neuromuscular signaling breaks down.

**Fluid and blood volume regulation.** Sodium is the primary determinant of extracellular fluid volume. It works with albumin to create osmotic pressure that draws excess fluid back into blood vessels, preventing edema while keeping organs perfused.

**Nutrient absorption.** Sodium is required at the cellular level for glucose and amino acids to be absorbed from the intestine — a foundational metabolic role.

**Stress hormone regulation.** Adequate sodium has a well-documented anti-adrenaline effect. When sodium is too low, adrenaline and cortisol rise to compensate for impaired glucose delivery. Restoring sodium reduces this compensatory stress response, which is why a small salty snack before bed can meaningfully improve sleep.

**Magnesium retention.** When sodium falls too low, the body raises aldosterone to conserve it — but aldosterone simultaneously drives losses of potassium, magnesium, and calcium. Adequate sodium short-circuits this cycle and helps preserve magnesium status, which is already commonly deficient.

**Protein sparing.** If sodium is insufficient to buffer urinary acid excretion, the kidneys break down protein to generate ammonium as a substitute. Adequate sodium thus helps preserve lean mass.

**Metabolic rate and thyroid support.** Sodium is considered thermogenic and supports metabolic rate alongside dietary protein, thyroid hormone, and progesterone.

**Fetal and brain development.** Sodium, alongside progesterone and glucose, is a key factor in brain development. Restriction during pregnancy is associated with insufficient blood volume to supply the uterus and fetus.

**Deficiency warning signs** include elevated stress hormones, insomnia, gut dysfunction, impaired insulin sensitivity, nocturia, edema, magnesium wasting, and paradoxically elevated blood pressure — driven by aldosterone and renin-angiotensin system (RAAS) activation from restriction, not from excess.

## How to get it

**Salting whole foods with quality sea salt is the gold standard.** Sea salt contains naturally occurring trace minerals and no artificial additives. Heavily processed iodized table salt is a lesser option. Himalayan pink salt may be problematic for those with concerns about iron overload due to its elevated iron content, so sea salt is generally preferred.

**Sodium-rich whole foods** include animal foods broadly, bone broth and stock, fermented foods (pickles, olives, miso, sauerkraut), shellfish, fish, and dairy — all of which deliver sodium alongside complementary minerals.

**Salted water** is a simple and effective strategy, particularly during illness, exercise, heat exposure, or stress. Adding a pinch of sea salt to a glass of water prevents over-dilution of electrolytes and supports stomach acid production.

**Electrolyte blends** containing sodium, potassium, and magnesium together are useful for athletes, heavy sweaters, those on low-carbohydrate or ketogenic diets (which cause the kidneys to excrete more sodium), and anyone under significant physical or psychological stress. Choose formulas without excessive sugar, artificial sweeteners, or unnecessary additives.

**Sodium bicarbonate (baking soda)** is another supplemental form with additional acid-buffering properties, sometimes used by athletes for performance.

**Sodium chloride tablets** (USP-grade, 1 g each) offer precise, convenient dosing for those who need measured supplementation — during illness, repletion, or high-demand circumstances.

**Sodium does not work in isolation.** Its most important dietary relationships are:
- **Potassium** — counterbalances sodium and regulates fluid distribution; aim for at least 3 g/day from whole foods (potatoes, avocados, coconut water, winter squash) and a practical potassium-to-sodium ratio of 2:1 or higher
- **Magnesium** — synergistic with sodium; low sodium drives magnesium wasting via aldosterone
- **Calcium** — also lost through aldosterone activation triggered by sodium restriction

Increase intake meaningfully during illness, heavy exercise, heat, pregnancy, stress, low-carbohydrate dieting, or poor sleep.

## Dosing

A practical daily sodium intake for most healthy adults falls in the range of **1.5–2.3 g of sodium per day** (roughly 3.8–5.8 g of sodium chloride/table salt) as a working baseline from food and discretionary salt combined. This is not a ceiling — it is a floor to ensure adequacy under normal conditions.

Higher intakes are appropriate and often necessary under conditions of elevated demand: heavy sweating, intense exercise, heat exposure, illness, pregnancy, ketogenic dieting, or chronic stress. In these circumstances, sodium losses increase substantially and intake should rise to match. Cravings for salty foods in these contexts are a real biological signal and should generally be honored.

The more important variable than the absolute sodium number is the **potassium-to-sodium balance**. Targeting at least 3 g of potassium daily alongside your sodium intake, and aiming for a 2:1 potassium-to-sodium ratio, is more protective for blood pressure and cardiovascular health than focusing on sodium restriction alone.

Extreme intakes in either direction are harmful. Very high sodium (approaching 20 g/day) is clearly excessive regardless of potassium status. Conversely, very low sodium combined with high fluid intake is also dangerous — it dilutes electrolytes, impairs stomach acid, and activates the RAAS in ways that raise blood pressure and drive further mineral losses. Drink water when thirsty rather than on a forced schedule, and add a pinch of sea salt to water when consuming large volumes.

## Risks

**Excess sodium without adequate potassium** is the primary risk scenario. High sodium combined with low potassium raises aldosterone, drives vasoconstriction, and elevates blood pressure. The fix is not sodium restriction alone but ensuring potassium and magnesium are sufficient alongside sodium.

**Extreme excess** (approaching 20 g/day of sodium) is clearly harmful regardless of mineral balance and should be avoided.

**Over-hydration relative to sodium** is an underappreciated risk. Drinking large quantities of water while sodium is low dilutes electrolytes (hyponatremia), impairs stomach acid production, and stresses the kidneys.

**Kidney disease.** Impaired sodium excretion in existing kidney disease may require careful management and individualized guidance — consult a clinician.

**Heart failure with fluid overload** is a distinct clinical scenario where sodium restriction may be appropriate under direct medical supervision.

**Drug and nutrient interactions.** Several important interactions apply:
- **Diuretics** (e.g., furosemide, hydrochlorothiazide) accelerate sodium and potassium excretion; those on diuretics should monitor electrolytes and may need to increase intake of both
- **ACE inhibitors and ARBs** alter sodium-potassium handling and RAAS activity; sodium intake should be discussed with the prescribing clinician
- **Lithium** (used in bipolar disorder) has a narrow therapeutic window that is highly sensitive to sodium status — low sodium causes lithium retention and toxicity risk; do not make large changes to sodium intake without medical supervision
- **NSAIDs** can cause sodium and fluid retention, elevating blood pressure — sodium intake in this context warrants awareness
- **Potassium-sparing diuretics or potassium supplements** combined with high sodium may require monitoring to maintain proper balance
- **Corticosteroids** promote sodium retention and potassium wasting, shifting the overall electrolyte balance
- Ensuring adequate **magnesium** is not merely a dietary nicety but an interaction of clinical relevance — low sodium drives magnesium wasting via aldosterone, worsening a deficiency that is already highly prevalent
