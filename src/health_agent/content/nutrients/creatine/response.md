## What it's for

Creatine is a naturally occurring compound synthesized in the body from the amino acids arginine, glycine, and methionine — primarily in the liver and kidneys. It is stored mainly in skeletal muscle and the brain as phosphocreatine, which acts as a rapid-recharge reservoir for ATP, the cell's primary energy currency. When ATP is depleted during intense effort or high cognitive demand, phosphocreatine donates a phosphate group to regenerate it almost instantaneously.

**Physical performance.** Creatine increases maximal power output during short, intense exercise — sprints, heavy lifting, and high-intensity intervals. It supports muscle protein synthesis, accelerates recovery between sets and sessions, and helps preserve lean mass.

**Cognitive function.** The brain is a high-ATP-demand organ, and phosphocreatine availability directly supports cognitive energy metabolism. Supplementation has shown cognitive benefits during sleep deprivation (at approximately 0.2 g/kg body weight acutely), boosts BDNF — a key factor in neuronal survival and plasticity — and is associated with neuroprotective effects in models of neurodegenerative disease.

**Mental health.** Five grams per day has been associated with 35–63% reductions in depression and anxiety scores within weeks, with higher remission rates when combined with standard antidepressants. Proposed mechanisms include improved mitochondrial energy availability in brain tissue and reduced oxidative stress.

**Mitochondrial and cellular health.** Creatine phosphate exists in equilibrium with ATP and may raise effective ATP concentrations without introducing excess adenosine. It reduces catecholamine-induced heart damage and supports mitochondrial function broadly.

**Homocysteine metabolism.** Creatine synthesis consumes methyl groups, so maintaining adequate creatine status reduces the methyl demand on the methylation cycle, potentially lowering homocysteine — relevant to cardiovascular and neurological health.

## How to get it

**Food sources.** Creatine is found almost exclusively in animal foods. The richest sources are red meat (beef, lamb), pork, poultry, and fish — particularly herring, salmon, and tuna. Cooking degrades creatine by up to 35%, so lightly cooked or rare preparations of beef and lightly cooked fish preserve the most. Vegetarians and vegans obtain virtually no dietary creatine and have significantly lower muscle and brain creatine stores, making supplementation especially impactful for this population.

**Best supplemental form.** Creatine monohydrate is the most researched, most validated, and most cost-effective form. Other marketed forms — creatine HCl, buffered creatine, ethyl ester — have not demonstrated superiority over monohydrate in well-controlled research and are typically more expensive. Creapure, a pharmaceutical-grade creatine monohydrate manufactured in Germany, is a widely recommended quality standard with rigorous purity testing. Avoid creatine gummies and many branded novelty formats — independent testing has found some contain little to no actual creatine despite label claims.

**Timing and absorption.** Timing is flexible — creatine is not acutely time-sensitive. Taking it with a carbohydrate-containing meal (breakfast, a post-workout meal, or juice) is practical and evidence-supported, as insulin facilitates creatine uptake into muscle. Consistency of daily intake matters more than precise timing. A pre-workout combination with baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) may offer additional buffering benefits during high-intensity efforts.

## Dosing

For most healthy adults, **5 g per day** is the standard maintenance dose, consistently cited for both physical performance and mental health benefits. This dose achieves full muscle saturation within approximately 3–4 weeks. Splitting into two doses of 5 g each is an alternative that may improve tolerability.

An optional **loading protocol** — 20 g per day divided into four doses for 5–7 days, followed by 5 g/day maintenance — saturates muscle stores more rapidly but is not necessary and may increase GI side effects in sensitive individuals.

For acute cognitive support during sleep deprivation, a situational dose of approximately **0.2 g/kg body weight** (for example, ~15 g for a 75 kg person) has shown benefit. This is not a daily maintenance recommendation — it is an acute, as-needed protocol distinct from the standard 5 g/day regimen.

Vegetarians, vegans, and older adults may notice larger performance and cognitive responses due to lower baseline stores.

## Risks

Creatine monohydrate has one of the strongest safety profiles of any supplement in the research literature. No serious adverse effects have been demonstrated in healthy individuals at standard doses.

**Common minor side effects.** GI discomfort — bloating, cramping, or loose stools — can occur, particularly during loading or with large single doses. Splitting the dose typically resolves this. Early water retention within muscle cells is a normal physiological response reflecting creatine uptake, not a harmful effect.

**Kidney concerns.** The idea that creatine damages healthy kidneys is not supported by credible evidence at recommended doses. However, individuals with pre-existing kidney disease should consult a physician before supplementing. Note that creatine supplementation naturally raises urinary and serum creatinine — a metabolite of creatine — which can appear to suggest kidney stress on standard lab panels. Inform your clinician of supplementation before kidney function is interpreted.

**Drug and nutrient interactions.** No major negative drug interactions have been identified in the available literature. Some older research suggested that large amounts of caffeine taken simultaneously may blunt creatine's ergogenic effects, though this remains debated. Creatine is tightly linked to methylation pathways; individuals with MTHFR variants, elevated homocysteine, or methylation dysfunction should pay attention to its interactions with TMG (trimethylglycine), B vitamins, and glycine, and may benefit from a broader methylation support approach. Creatine itself may reduce methylation burden by lowering the methyl demand of endogenous creatine synthesis.

**Product quality risk.** The FDA does not require supplements to be proven safe or effective before market entry. Independent testing has found numerous creatine products containing significantly less creatine than labeled. Prioritize Creapure-certified or independently third-party-tested products to avoid this risk.
