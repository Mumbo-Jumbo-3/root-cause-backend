## What it's for

Vitamin D functions more like a steroid hormone than a traditional vitamin, regulating an estimated 1,000 or more genes and influencing nearly every major system in the body.

**Skeletal and mineral health:** Vitamin D promotes calcium and phosphate absorption in the gut, supports bone growth and remodeling, and prevents hypocalcemic tetany. It works synergistically with vitamin K2, which directs calcium into bones rather than arteries.

**Hormonal and metabolic function:** It plays a role in sex hormone production (testosterone, progesterone), regulates insulin and glucose metabolism, modulates leptin (hunger and satiety signaling), and supports thyroid function — deficiency can suppress the hypothalamus-pituitary-thyroid axis and slow metabolic rate.

**Immune regulation:** Vitamin D modulates both innate and adaptive immunity, activates antimicrobial peptides in macrophages, and is associated with reduced risk of autoimmune disease and systemic inflammation.

**Neurological and cognitive health:** It influences brain function, circadian rhythm regulation, and is associated with reduced risk of cognitive decline and Alzheimer's disease.

**Additional roles:** Muscle preservation, blood pressure regulation, fertility support, skin and gut health, and detoxification of xenobiotics.

The standard measure of vitamin D status is a serum **25(OH)D** blood test. Optimal levels are generally considered to be **45–70 ng/mL**; below 20 ng/mL represents serious deficiency, and above 70 ng/mL is considered excessive.

## How to get it

**Sunlight — the primary and preferred source**

Vitamin D is synthesized in the skin when UVB rays convert 7-dehydrocholesterol to previtamin D3. Aim for 30–120 minutes of direct skin exposure daily, ideally between midday and early afternoon when UVB intensity is highest. Exposure through glass does not work. The body self-limits synthesis from sunlight, so toxicity from sun exposure is not a concern. Adequate zinc, vitamin C, magnesium, and dietary cholesterol are needed to support efficient synthesis and conversion.

Common reasons people remain deficient despite sun availability include indoor lifestyles, sunscreen use, high-fiber or low-fat diets, magnesium deficiency, high caffeine intake, and geographic latitude in winter months. If sunlight is genuinely inaccessible for extended periods, a UVB vitamin D lamp is a reasonable alternative.

**Food sources**

Food alone rarely achieves optimal levels, but the best dietary contributors include:
- Cod liver (in its own oil) — also provides synergistic vitamin A
- Wild-caught fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring)
- Shellfish, seafood (skin, roe, eyes)
- UV-exposed mushrooms
- Egg yolks
- Organ meats (liver, heart)
- Grass-fed meats, dairy, and butter

Whole food sources are preferred over supplements when accessible, as they naturally provide co-factors (retinol, K2, dietary fat) that support absorption and utilization.

**Supplements**

Consider supplementing when blood levels remain low after two months of optimizing sun exposure and diet, or when seasonal and geographic constraints make adequate sun exposure impossible.

- **Use vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol)** — the form naturally produced in skin and found in animal foods. Avoid D2 (ergocalciferol), which is less effective and may lower D3 levels.
- **Choose an oil-based or liquid preparation** — D3 is fat-soluble and absorbs best in an oil carrier (olive oil, MCT oil).
- **Take in the morning or around midday** with a fat-containing meal. Avoid evening dosing, as vitamin D opposes melatonin production and may disrupt sleep.
- **Always pair with vitamin K2 (MK-7 form)** to direct calcium to bones rather than arteries — approximately 100–150 mcg MK-7 per 1,000 IU D3. Note that MK-7 has a long half-life and may be cycled rather than taken every single day.
- **Ensure magnesium adequacy** — magnesium is required for vitamin D activation and deficiency is a primary cause of poor conversion. Do not take large doses of magnesium and vitamin D at the same time; stagger them.
- **Vitamin A (retinol)** from whole foods (eggs, liver, grass-fed dairy) works synergistically with D3; avoid chronic over-supplementation of vitamin A, as imbalance relative to D can impair calcium transport.

## Dosing

Start by testing your serum 25(OH)D level — this determines which range applies to you.

**Baseline daily intake from food and moderate sun exposure** may provide roughly 400–1,000 IU/day for individuals with good habits and favorable conditions, but this is rarely sufficient to reach or maintain optimal blood levels (45–70 ng/mL) for most people.

**When supplementing:**
- **Maintenance at adequate levels:** 1,000–2,000 IU/day
- **General optimization for most adults:** 2,000–5,000 IU/day
- **Correcting confirmed deficiency:** Up to 5,000–8,000 IU/day initially, under monitoring
- **Conservative unsupervised upper limit:** ~4,000 IU/day without close testing

Retest serum 25(OH)D after approximately one month of supplementation and adjust dose to reach and stay within the 45–70 ng/mL range. Do not supplement indefinitely at high doses without ongoing monitoring. Individuals with kidney dysfunction require medical supervision, as impaired kidney function directly affects vitamin D activation.

## Risks

**Toxicity:** Vitamin D toxicity (hypervitaminosis D) is uncommon at standard doses but real with prolonged high-dose supplementation without monitoring. It does not occur from sun exposure, as the body self-limits UVB-driven synthesis. Toxicity manifests primarily as hypercalcemia (elevated blood calcium), with symptoms including nausea, weakness, frequent urination, and in severe or chronic cases, kidney damage. Keeping blood levels below 70 ng/mL and supplementing K2 alongside D3 are the key safety measures.

**Upper limits:** The conservative unsupervised daily limit is around 4,000 IU/day. Higher doses are used clinically for deficiency correction but require blood test monitoring. The safety margin at 2,000–5,000 IU/day is wide for most healthy adults.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **Seizure medications (e.g., phenytoin, phenobarbital):** Clinically significant interaction — these drugs accelerate vitamin D metabolism; consult a physician before supplementing.
- **Kidney disease:** Impairs activation of vitamin D — requires medical supervision.
- **Vitamin K2:** Synergistic and considered an essential safety co-factor when supplementing D3; ensures calcium is directed to bones rather than arterial walls.
- **Magnesium:** Required for vitamin D activation; deficiency undermines D status regardless of intake. Stagger large magnesium doses away from D3.
- **Vitamin A (retinol):** Synergistic in appropriate balance; chronic excess of vitamin A relative to D can impair calcium transport genes and cause bone issues. Prefer food sources over high-dose retinol supplements.
- **High caffeine intake:** May impair vitamin D metabolism; reducing intake can improve conversion.
- **High-fiber diets:** Can reduce intestinal absorption of vitamin D.
- **Low-fat or low-cholesterol diets:** Impair both skin synthesis and intestinal absorption of this fat-soluble vitamin.
- **Zinc or vitamin C deficiency:** May impair UVB-driven skin synthesis.
