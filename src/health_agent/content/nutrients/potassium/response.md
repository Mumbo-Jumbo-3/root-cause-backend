## What it's for

Potassium is an essential mineral and the third most abundant mineral in the human body. It functions as an electrolyte — carrying an electrical charge in solution — and underpins several critical physiological processes:

- **Fluid and cellular balance:** Potassium is the primary electrolyte inside cells, governing how much water cells retain. It works in opposition to sodium, which governs fluid outside cells. The sodium-potassium balance is foundational to virtually every cell in the body.
- **Heart and muscle function:** Every heartbeat depends on proper potassium levels. It drives the electrical gradients that enable both cardiac and skeletal muscle contraction.
- **Blood pressure regulation:** Potassium helps the kidneys excrete excess sodium and relaxes blood vessel walls, directly countering hypertension.
- **Nerve signaling:** The sodium-potassium pump maintains the electrical gradients required for nerve impulse transmission.
- **Glucose metabolism:** Potassium plays an underappreciated role in how cells take up and process glucose, and deficiency can impair glucose handling in ways that resemble insulin resistance.
- **Kidney health:** Adequate potassium supports normal kidney function and urinary health.
- **Stress resilience:** Potassium is lost during illness, intense exercise, and heavy sweating; depletion during these periods can accelerate fatigue, cramping, and cardiovascular strain.

## How to get it

**Food is strongly preferred over supplements.** Potassium from whole foods arrives with synergistic fiber, vitamins, and co-factors that improve utilization and make toxicity essentially impossible at normal dietary intakes.

**Top food sources:**
- **Avocados** — one of the highest-potassium foods available, also rich in fiber and healthy fats
- **Potatoes** (especially baked or well-cooked) — excellent source; if starch is a concern, pre-soak sliced potatoes in water with ½ tsp aluminum-free baking soda for 5 minutes before cooking
- **Winter squash** — high potassium, broadly well tolerated
- **Bananas** — convenient, practical, and well known
- **Coconut water** — a particularly useful electrolyte drink delivering potassium alongside sodium and magnesium in a naturally balanced ratio; 100–200 ml daily is a practical, gentle dose
- **Whole fruits and fruit juices** — broadly good sources across the category
- **Leafy greens and other whole plant foods** — potassium is found throughout the solid parts of plants
- **Dairy** — a modest but consistent contributor

**Potassium-to-sodium ratio matters as much as absolute intake.** A minimum 1:1 potassium-to-sodium ratio is a reasonable baseline; a 2:1 ratio (potassium:sodium) is more optimal for most people. Modern processed-food diets invert this ratio, which is a primary driver of potassium-related health issues.

**If supplementing is necessary**, acceptable forms include:
- Potassium chloride powder (e.g., Morton's Salt Substitute — confirm it contains no ammonium chloride)
- Potassium citrate or potassium gluconate (gentler on digestion)
- Potassium bicarbonate
- Cream of tartar (potassium bitartrate) — a low-risk kitchen staple
- Potassium-based salt replacements such as Lo Salt or Nu Salt

**Critical rules for supplemental use:** Never take potassium chloride in tablet form — high local concentration in the gut can arrest intestinal motility and ulcerate the intestinal lining. Always use powder form, sprinkled on or mixed with food. Take small amounts spread across the day, always with meals.

## Dosing

For most healthy adults, the adequate intake (AI) for potassium is approximately **2,600 mg/day for women and 3,400 mg/day for men**, as set by major health authorities. A diet built around potassium-rich whole foods — potatoes, avocados, winter squash, bananas, coconut water, and fruits — will typically meet or exceed this target without supplementation.

When supplementing to address a gap or support higher-demand situations (intense exercise, low-carb dieting, illness, heavy sweating), **start with small doses — 100–200 mg elemental potassium per serving** — taken with food and spread across the day. Most over-the-counter supplements are capped at 99–200 mg per serving by convention precisely because higher concentrated doses carry cardiovascular risk. Individuals on ketogenic or very low-carbohydrate diets lose more potassium via the kidneys due to reduced insulin and increased urinary excretion, and should proactively monitor and replenish intake. For therapeutic repletion beyond everyday food and a modest supplement, work with a healthcare provider who can guide dosing based on bloodwork.

A practical daily anchor: **100–200 ml of coconut water** in the morning or after activity provides a gentle, well-tolerated electrolyte foundation that can be combined with dietary sources rather than relying on concentrated supplements.

## Risks

**Upper limits and overdose:** The tolerable upper limit for potassium from supplements and fortified foods is **not formally established for healthy adults** because healthy kidneys efficiently excrete excess. However, high-dose supplementation — particularly in concentrated or tablet form — can raise blood potassium (hyperkalemia) to levels that cause dangerous cardiac arrhythmia, including potentially fatal slowing or arrest of the heart. This risk is real and should not be minimized.

**Kidney disease — the most critical contraindication:** Individuals with impaired kidney function must not freely supplement potassium in any concentrated form. When the kidneys cannot adequately excrete potassium, it accumulates rapidly, creating life-threatening hyperkalemia. Anyone with kidney disease must work with a clinician before increasing potassium intake.

**Gastrointestinal effects:** Potassium supplements — especially potassium chloride — can cause nausea, stomach discomfort, and GI irritation. Taking them in powder form mixed with food, rather than as tablets or in a concentrated liquid bolus, substantially reduces this risk. Those with gut sensitivity may tolerate potassium citrate or small amounts of coconut water with glycine (1–2 g) better than chloride forms.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **Diuretics:** Thiazide and loop diuretics increase urinary potassium loss, raising deficiency risk; potassium-sparing diuretics (e.g., spironolactone, amiloride) do the opposite, raising hyperkalemia risk if potassium is also supplemented.
- **ACE inhibitors and ARBs:** Commonly used for blood pressure and heart conditions, these drugs reduce potassium excretion — combining them with potassium supplements can push blood levels dangerously high.
- **NSAIDs:** Can impair renal potassium excretion, compounding risk in people also taking ACE inhibitors or supplementing potassium.
- **Digoxin:** Potassium levels critically affect digoxin toxicity risk; both low and high potassium are dangerous in this context.
- **Magnesium:** Low magnesium impairs the body's ability to retain potassium intracellularly; if potassium repletion is slow or incomplete, check magnesium status as well.

Anyone taking prescription medications that affect blood pressure, heart rhythm, or kidney function should consult their healthcare provider before using potassium supplements.
