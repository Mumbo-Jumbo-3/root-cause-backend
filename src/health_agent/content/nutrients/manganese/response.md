## What it's for

Manganese is an essential trace mineral stored primarily in the bones, liver, kidneys, and pancreas. It serves several critical physiological roles:

**Antioxidant defense:** Manganese is a core structural component of Manganese Superoxide Dismutase (Mn-SOD), a key antioxidant enzyme operating inside the mitochondria. Because mitochondria consume over 90% of the oxygen cells use, Mn-SOD is essential for neutralizing superoxide radicals and protecting mitochondrial integrity.

**Connective tissue and bone formation:** Manganese is required for synthesizing collagen, glycosaminoglycans, bone matrix proteins, and blood clotting factors.

**Metabolism:** It supports fat and carbohydrate metabolism, calcium absorption, and blood sugar regulation relevant to insulin sensitivity.

**Hormonal and reproductive health:** Manganese is needed for sex hormone production and GnRH (Gonadotropin-releasing hormone) synthesis, making it important for fertility in both men and women.

**Neurological function:** It supports neurotransmitter activity and myelin integrity, and is necessary for normal brain and nerve function.

**Thyroid function:** Manganese is required for the synthesis of thyroxin (T4); deficiency may impair thyroid output.

**Urea cycle:** The manganese-dependent enzyme arginase converts arginine to ornithine and urea, supporting nitrogen metabolism and detoxification.

## How to get it

**Food first — dietary deficiency is uncommon** in people eating a varied whole-food diet, and food sources are the preferred route.

Top practical sources include:

- **Mussels and oysters** — among the richest sources; weekly consumption comfortably covers needs
- **Organic maple syrup** — small amounts provide meaningful manganese
- **Organic cacao** — occasional use effectively contributes to intake
- **Pineapple** — notably high in manganese
- **Oats** — a reliable plant-based source
- **Potatoes** — accessible everyday source (~0.3 mg each)
- **Leafy greens** (spinach, kale) — decent amounts, though oxalates may reduce absorption
- **Lion's Mane mushroom** — considered a particularly rich and underrated dietary source
- **Bee pollen** — approximately 10 mg per 50 g serving
- **Coffee** — a meaningful passive source for regular drinkers
- **Nuts, seeds, and whole grains** — contain manganese but also phytates that reduce bioavailability

A practical weekly pattern of shellfish, occasional cacao, maple syrup, pineapple, oats, and potatoes in a varied diet will meet manganese needs for most people.

**Supplementation** is rarely necessary but may be warranted if the diet is severely restricted. When supplementing, **manganese bisglycinate** (chelated) is generally considered the most bioavailable and gentlest form. Manganese gluconate and manganese aspartate/citrate are also well-absorbed alternatives. Powders, liquids, and capsules are all viable depending on preference, provided the product is free of unnecessary fillers and additives.

## Dosing

**Baseline daily intake from food:** The Adequate Intake (AI) is approximately **2.3 mg/day for men** and **1.8 mg/day for women**. A varied whole-food diet typically meets this without supplementation.

**If supplementing:** A range of **1–5 mg/day of elemental manganese** is generally sufficient to address dietary gaps or periods of higher demand. There is no established benefit to exceeding this range through supplementation. The Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) from combined food and supplement sources is **11 mg/day** for adults — stay well below this ceiling when supplementing.

Do not megadose. Manganese is a nutrient where getting enough from food is both achievable and preferable, and more is not better.

## Risks

**Toxicity from food alone is extremely rare.** Risks of excess are primarily associated with:

- **High-dose or prolonged supplementation**
- **Contaminated drinking water** in some regions
- **Inhalation exposure** in occupational settings (welders, miners) — this can cause **manganism**, a serious neurological disorder resembling Parkinson's disease, characterized by tremors, cognitive impairment, mood changes, and motor dysfunction
- **Parenteral (IV) nutrition** without careful monitoring, particularly in neonates

**Populations at higher risk of accumulation:**
- People with **liver disease**, due to impaired excretion
- Those with **iron deficiency** — iron and manganese share absorption pathways, so low iron significantly increases manganese uptake and raises the risk of accumulation

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **Iron:** Competes directly for absorption; iron deficiency increases manganese absorption and accumulation risk
- **Calcium and phosphorus:** High intakes of either may reduce manganese absorption
- **Magnesium:** May compete for absorption at higher doses
- **Phytic acid** (found in grains, legumes, nuts): Reduces manganese bioavailability from plant sources
- **Turkey Tail mushroom:** Long-term use has been associated with depletion of trace minerals including manganese; avoid extended use
- **Thyroid medications:** Because manganese supports thyroxin synthesis, significant deficiency or excess could theoretically interact with thyroid function; consult a clinician if you are managing thyroid conditions
