## What it's for

Iron is an essential mineral with several critical roles in human physiology. Its most well-known function is **oxygen transport**: iron is the core component of hemoglobin in red blood cells and myoglobin in muscle tissue, enabling the body to carry and store oxygen. Beyond this, iron is required for cellular energy production and mitochondrial function, and it acts as a cofactor for dozens of enzymes throughout the body.

Iron is also directly involved in **dopamine synthesis** — it is a required cofactor for tyrosine hydroxylase, the enzyme that produces dopamine — making it relevant to mood, motivation, and cognitive function. It supports immune cell activity, red blood cell production, and hair health: ferritin levels below approximately 40 ng/mL are associated with hair thinning and loss.

Critically, iron is one of the few nutrients where **both deficiency and excess cause serious harm**. The body has virtually no active mechanism to excrete surplus iron. When iron accumulates in organs — liver, heart, pancreas, joints, skin — it catalyzes the Fenton reaction, generating highly reactive hydroxyl radicals that drive oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and organ damage. Hereditary hemochromatosis (caused by HFE gene mutations, particularly C282Y and H63D) eliminates the normal absorption gatekeeper entirely, making chronic overload a significant and underappreciated modern health risk.

## How to get it

**Food is the preferred source.** There are two main dietary forms:

- **Heme iron** (animal sources) has 15–35% bioavailability and is absorbed directly via dedicated intestinal transporters, largely unaffected by dietary inhibitors. Best sources: beef liver, red meat (beef, lamb, bison), oysters and shellfish, sardines, and dark poultry meat. Oysters and shellfish also provide copper, which is important for iron regulation.
- **Non-heme iron** (plant sources) has 2–20% bioavailability, is more affected by dietary context, and is strongly enhanced by pairing with vitamin C. Sources include lentils, beans, chickpeas, spinach, dark leafy greens, cacao, molasses, and bran. Absorption is inhibited by tannins in coffee and tea, calcium from dairy, and phytates in grains and legumes — which can be strategically useful if stores are adequate.

**Lactoferrin**, a glycoprotein found in milk and colostrum, is worth highlighting as a whole-food option. It acts as an intelligent iron regulator — enhancing absorption when stores are low, preventing overload when stores are high, reducing iron-driven inflammation, and competing with pathogens for available iron.

**Supplementation** should only be considered with lab-confirmed deficiency (low ferritin, low transferrin saturation, and low hemoglobin together). If supplementation is warranted, preferred forms are:
- **Ferrous bisglycinate** (iron glycinate) — gentle on the GI tract, good bioavailability
- **Ferrous gluconate** — gentler than ferrous sulfate
- **Heme iron polypeptide** — derived from animal hemoglobin, high bioavailability
- **Lactoferrin** — supports absorption intelligently without forcing it

Avoid ferrous sulfate (harsh, more oxidative stress), elemental iron in fortified foods (poorly regulated by the body), and multivitamins combining iron with high-dose vitamin C and copper, which can drive oxidative damage.

To moderate absorption if stores are adequate: drink coffee or tea with iron-rich meals, include some dairy, and avoid large vitamin C doses alongside those meals. To support healthy iron regulation overall, ensure adequate copper, zinc, and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), which all play roles in iron metabolism.

## Dosing

The baseline daily intake target from food is approximately **8 mg/day for adult men and postmenopausal women**, and **18 mg/day for premenopausal women**, reflecting monthly losses from menstruation.

These food-based targets are best met through a diet emphasizing heme iron from animal sources. Plant-based eaters should pair non-heme iron foods with vitamin C and monitor ferritin levels, since bioavailability is substantially lower.

**If supplementation is clinically indicated** (confirmed by ferritin, transferrin saturation, and hemoglobin labs), supplemental doses typically range from **18–29 mg/day** of elemental iron in a chelated form such as ferrous bisglycinate. Do not supplement without lab confirmation — iron supplementation in someone with adequate or elevated iron stores causes direct harm. If supplementing, take with food to reduce GI irritation, avoid large amounts of caffeine alongside the dose (it impairs absorption), and avoid taking iron at the same time as vitamin E (iron destroys it in the stomach — take them separately). Reassess labs after no more than 4 weeks of unsupervised supplementation. Pregnancy increases iron needs, but routine supplementation without confirmed deficiency is not justified.

## Risks

**Side effects** from supplemental iron include GI irritation, nausea, constipation, and dark stools — especially with ferrous sulfate. Chelated forms (bisglycinate, gluconate) are substantially gentler.

**Upper limit and overload risk**: The tolerable upper intake level for adults is 45 mg/day from all sources. Because the body cannot actively excrete iron, chronic overconsumption — whether from excessive supplementation, fortified foods, or genetic susceptibility — leads to tissue iron deposition, oxidative stress via the Fenton reaction, liver disease, cardiomyopathy, pancreatic damage (increasing diabetes risk), joint disease, and hormonal disruption. Immune function is also impaired: pathogens including bacteria, candida, and malaria parasites thrive on available iron. People with HFE gene mutations (C282Y and H63D, most common in those of Northern European descent) are at particularly high risk. Regular blood donation (2–4 times per year) is the most effective strategy to reduce elevated iron stores.

**Drug and nutrient interactions**:
- **Vitamin C** significantly enhances non-heme iron absorption — avoid pairing with iron-rich meals if stores are already adequate
- **Calcium** inhibits both heme and non-heme iron absorption
- **Copper deficiency** causes tissues to retain excess iron; adequate copper (from oysters, liver, shellfish) is essential for proper iron regulation
- **Zinc** competes with iron at the DMT1 intestinal transporter
- **Tannins** (coffee, tea) substantially inhibit non-heme iron absorption — protective when stores are sufficient
- **Vitamin E** is destroyed by iron in the stomach — take them at separate times
- **Phytates** in grains and legumes reduce non-heme iron absorption
- **Iron shares the DMT1 transporter with toxic metals** including lead, aluminum, and cadmium — adequate iron status helps crowd out these competitors, while iron deficiency may increase heavy metal absorption
- **Antacids and proton pump inhibitors** reduce stomach acid and can impair iron absorption
- **Certain antibiotics** (quinolones, tetracyclines) bind iron and should be taken apart from iron supplements by at least two hours
