## What it's for

Tryptophan is an essential amino acid — meaning the body cannot make it and must obtain it from food or supplements — that serves as the foundational precursor to both serotonin and melatonin. Serotonin availability shapes mood, learning, and alertness, while melatonin governs sleep cycles, heavy-metal binding, and reproductive hormone signaling via GnRH. Tryptophan also directly inhibits thyroid peroxidase, meaning its dietary level influences thyroid hormone output, testosterone, and growth hormone in ways that are not always intuitive: excess tryptophan, by driving serotonin production, can suppress anabolic and thyroid hormones rather than support them. Insufficient intake contributes to depression, anxiety, irritability, and disrupted sleep, and deficiency is compounded by normal aging, which progressively reduces tryptophan availability.

## How to get it

**Food sources** (ranked roughly by tryptophan density):
- Turkey, tuna, chicken
- Eggs (whole — egg whites alone are rich in tryptophan, but discarding the yolk removes valuable cofactors)
- Dairy: whole milk and whey-containing products carry more tryptophan than cheese, because cheese is made by removing whey
- Chia seeds, nuts, and seeds
- Dark chocolate

**Supplement forms:**
- **L-tryptophan** — the direct free amino acid; the most studied form and precursor to both serotonin and melatonin
- **5-HTP (5-hydroxytryptophan)** — one metabolic step closer to serotonin; crosses the blood-brain barrier more readily than L-tryptophan, making it useful when central serotonin support is the primary goal
- **Combination formulas/patches** — commonly pair tryptophan with melatonin, ashwagandha, or magnesium; note that co-formulated synthetic melatonin carries its own risks (see Risks)

**Cofactors that determine conversion efficiency:**
- **Magnesium** — required by tryptophan hydroxylase for serotonin synthesis
- **Iron, BH4, and oxygen** — additional cofactors for the same enzyme
- **Thiamine (B1)** — deficiency paradoxically increases serotonin production, skewing tryptophan partitioning; short-term repletion may help normalize the pathway
- **Vitamin D** — activates the TPH2 gene, enabling tryptophan-to-serotonin conversion in the brain

Without adequate cofactors, increasing tryptophan intake produces unreliable results regardless of dose.

## Dosing

**Baseline from food:** The WHO estimates the adult maintenance requirement at roughly **4 mg/kg/day** — approximately **280 mg/day** for a 70 kg adult. A diet meeting the protein RDA (0.8 g/kg/day) from varied sources typically supplies this automatically, since tryptophan makes up about 1–1.5% of dietary protein.

**Supplemental range:** Pure L-tryptophan is considered safe at **500–3,000 mg/day** with appropriate cofactors. Use 500–1,000 mg for modest mood or sleep support; reserve 1,500–3,000 mg for therapeutic repletion, divided across the day rather than taken as a single dose.

**Timing matters:**
- *Sleep/melatonin support:* Take 30–60 minutes before bed; nocturnal conditions favor melatonin synthesis
- *Mood/serotonin support:* Take in the morning or midday, ideally on an empty stomach or with a small carbohydrate serving — insulin clears competing amino acids from the bloodstream, improving tryptophan's access to the brain

**Cofactor pairing:** When supplementing, consider 50–150 mg magnesium and a 2–4 week thiamine course to optimize conversion efficiency.

## Risks

**Side effects** at typical supplemental doses are generally mild: nausea, stomach upset, diarrhea, and occasional rash. At higher doses or with prolonged use, excess gut serotonin can worsen GI inflammation. High experimental doses in animal models produced connective-tissue changes and inflammatory cell proliferation in lung, spleen, and muscle tissue.

**Eosinophilia-Myalgia Syndrome (EMS):** A 1980s–90s outbreak linked L-tryptophan supplements to EMS, a serious multisystemic illness. Subsequent investigation identified a specific manufacturing contaminant (1,1'-ethylidene-bis-L-tryptophan) as the primary cause, not tryptophan itself. This history makes sourcing from manufacturers that explicitly test for Peak-X and related contaminants, with third-party certificates of analysis (COAs), non-negotiable.

**Tolerable upper limit:** No formal UL has been established by U.S. authorities, partly because EMS data confounded safety analysis. Research-supported practice treats **3,000 mg/day** as the practical ceiling for healthy adults.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs:** Combining tryptophan or 5-HTP with these drugs significantly raises serotonin syndrome risk — a potentially life-threatening emergency. Avoid without direct medical supervision.
- **Blood thinners and diabetes medications:** Potential interactions exist; consult a clinician before supplementing.
- **Thyroid medications:** High-dose tryptophan inhibits thyroid peroxidase and may interfere with thyroid hormone therapies.
- **Co-formulated synthetic melatonin:** Unless treating acute illness, synthetic melatonin can suppress α-MSH and has been associated with autoimmune risk — a reason to scrutinize combination products carefully.
