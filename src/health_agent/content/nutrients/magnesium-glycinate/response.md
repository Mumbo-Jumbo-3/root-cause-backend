## What it's for

Magnesium glycinate is a chelated form of magnesium — elemental magnesium bound to glycine, an amino acid — that delivers benefits from both components. Magnesium participates in over 3,700 enzymatic reactions in the body, making it foundational to virtually every system. Glycine adds its own calming, inhibitory neurotransmitter support on top of that.

Key roles include:

- **Energy production:** Required for every ATP-generating reaction — essentially all cellular energy
- **Sleep:** Enhances GABA activity and, alongside glycine, supports the brain's glymphatic waste-clearance system during deep sleep
- **Nervous system:** Regulates nerve signaling and protects against excess excitation via NMDA receptor antagonism
- **Muscle function:** Supports relaxation, reduces cramps and spasms, and helps maintain a steady heartbeat
- **Bone health:** Over half of the body's magnesium is stored in bone
- **Hormonal and metabolic:** Lowers SHBG (increasing free testosterone), improves insulin sensitivity, and is required to activate and metabolize vitamin D
- **Antioxidant support:** Glycine is a precursor to glutathione

Deficiency is widespread due to soil depletion, processed food diets, chronic stress (which rapidly depletes magnesium), and poor sleep — each worsening the others in a reinforcing cycle. Common signs of deficiency include poor sleep, muscle cramps or twitches, fatigue, anxiety, headaches or migraines, heart palpitations, blood sugar dysregulation, and constipation.

The glycinate form is preferred for daily use because its absorption rate is significantly higher than magnesium oxide (which is roughly 4% bioavailable) and generally comparable to or better than citrate, while being markedly gentler on the gastrointestinal tract.

## How to get it

**From food:** Whole food sources of magnesium include cacao and dark chocolate (one of the richest sources), mineral water, dark leafy greens such as spinach and Swiss chard, pumpkin seeds, almonds, cashews, black beans, edamame, and whole grains. Note that phytates in grains can reduce absorption, and modern soil depletion reduces the magnesium content of plant foods broadly. Most people cannot reliably meet optimal magnesium needs from food alone, particularly under stress or with high physical demand.

**From supplements — oral:** Magnesium glycinate capsules or powder are the most practical daily form. Powder dissolved in water absorbs slightly faster than capsules and can also be taken sublingually (held briefly under the tongue) for faster nervous system delivery — useful for acute anxiety, panic, or pre-sleep use. Take with food if GI sensitivity is a concern, and divide doses throughout the day rather than taking everything at once.

**From supplements — topical:** Magnesium chloride spray or solution applied to the back of the neck, shoulders, and upper body bypasses the GI tract entirely. This is especially useful for acute calming, muscle tension, or when oral intake is limited. Magnesium sulfate (Epsom salt) baths offer a similar transdermal route and are useful for muscle recovery and relaxation.

**Combining forms and routes:** Different forms preferentially reach different tissues. A practical approach combines magnesium glycinate orally (targeting nervous system and muscle tissue) with magnesium chloride topically or dissolved in drinking water throughout the day for broader systemic coverage.

**Synergistic pairings:**
- **L-theanine (1:1 ratio):** Highly synergistic for GABA support, sleep quality, and nervous system calming
- **Vitamin D:** Magnesium is required to activate vitamin D; neither works optimally without the other
- **Taurine (250–2,000 mg):** Enhances GABA-chloride function; magnesium taurate is a combined option for those who don't tolerate glycine well
- **Potassium and sodium:** Deficiencies in these electrolytes frequently co-occur with magnesium deficiency and are mutually reinforcing

## Dosing

Magnesium glycinate contains roughly 10–14% elemental magnesium by weight, so a 400 mg glycinate dose delivers approximately 40–56 mg of elemental magnesium.

**Baseline intake from food** should be the foundation. Adults generally need around 310–420 mg of elemental magnesium daily from all sources combined (varying by age and sex), an amount most people fall short of through diet alone.

**Supplemental dosing** closes that gap and can go higher when there is increased demand — chronic stress, intense exercise, poor sleep, or identified deficiency. A practical integrated framework:

- **Start with 200–400 mg magnesium glycinate** orally to assess tolerance, ideally split across two doses
- **Build toward a target** of approximately 5–10 mg of elemental magnesium per pound of body weight per day, distributed across all forms and routes combined — for a 150 lb person, this means 750–1,500 mg of elemental magnesium total daily
- **Find your personal bowel tolerance** — the dose just below causing loose stools — and treat that as your practical upper limit for oral intake on any given day

**A practical daily structure for a ~150 lb adult:**
- Morning: 400 mg magnesium glycinate (with or after breakfast)
- Afternoon or mid-day: 400 mg magnesium glycinate
- 30–60 minutes before bed: 500–1,000 mg magnesium glycinate (the highest single dose, to support sleep)
- Throughout the day: magnesium chloride dissolved in drinking water for additional systemic coverage
- Topical magnesium chloride spray to neck and shoulders as needed

Divide doses throughout the day to maintain steadier tissue levels and minimize GI upset. For sleep specifically, the pre-bed dose is most important. For acute muscle tension or anxiety, topical application or sublingual powder can be used on the spot.

**Testing:** RBC (red blood cell) magnesium is a far more accurate indicator of tissue status than standard serum magnesium, which remains normal until deficiency is severe.

## Risks

**General safety:** Magnesium glycinate is considered very safe in people with healthy kidneys. The most common effect of excessive oral intake is loose stools or diarrhea — the body's natural safety valve — which resolves quickly when the dose is reduced.

**Kidney disease:** This is the most important caution. Impaired kidneys cannot efficiently excrete excess magnesium, creating a genuine risk of hypermagnesemia (dangerously elevated magnesium levels). Anyone with renal impairment or kidney disease must use magnesium supplements only under medical supervision.

**GI sensitivity:** If magnesium causes immediate loose stools upon starting, this may signal co-deficiencies in thiamine (B1), sodium, or potassium. Start low, take with food, divide doses, and consider beginning with topical application while the oral dose is slowly increased.

**Upper limits:** The established tolerable upper intake level for supplemental magnesium in healthy adults is 350 mg of elemental magnesium per day from supplements alone, per most regulatory bodies. Higher intakes are sometimes used therapeutically but should be approached gradually and with awareness of bowel tolerance.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **Antibiotics (fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines):** Magnesium binds to these drugs and reduces their absorption. Separate doses by at least 2 hours.
- **Bisphosphonates** (e.g., alendronate for osteoporosis): Same absorption interference — separate dosing by 2+ hours.
- **Diuretics:** Loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide) increase magnesium excretion, worsening deficiency; potassium-sparing diuretics reduce excretion. Monitor levels with either type.
- **Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs):** Long-term PPI use impairs magnesium absorption and is a common cause of deficiency. Supplementation may be especially important but should be discussed with a clinician.
- **Calcium supplements:** High-dose calcium competes with magnesium for absorption. Do not take large calcium supplements without ensuring adequate magnesium; the two should be balanced.
- **Diabetes medications:** Magnesium improves insulin sensitivity and may amplify the glucose-lowering effect of medications like metformin or insulin. Monitor blood sugar when adjusting magnesium intake.
