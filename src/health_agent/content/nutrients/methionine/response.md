## What it's for

Methionine is an essential amino acid that sits at the center of one-carbon metabolism. Its most critical downstream product is S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe), the body's primary methyl donor, which supports nearly 100 biological processes including DNA and RNA synthesis, neurotransmitter regulation (via the COMT enzyme for dopamine, epinephrine, and norepinephrine), myelin sheath production, CoQ10 synthesis, and epigenetic gene expression. Methionine also serves as the starting point for glutathione — the body's master antioxidant — and creatine synthesis. Deficiency leads to liver damage, immune weakness, mood and cognitive problems, muscle fatigue, and hair and skin issues. Excess is equally problematic: too much methionine elevates homocysteine, inhibits thyroid function, damages mitochondria, and accelerates biological aging.

## How to get it

**Food sources** are the preferred route for most people. Methionine is abundant in:
- Animal proteins: muscle meat, fish, eggs, cheese, lean poultry
- Plant sources (lower but meaningful): rice, soy, legumes

Because methionine metabolism depends heavily on cofactors, dietary strategy matters as much as raw methionine intake:

- **Glycine balance:** A high-methionine diet without adequate glycine or proline disrupts methylation and raises homocysteine. Gelatin, bone broth, and quality organ meats are practical glycine sources. Supplemental glycine at 3–5 g/day can help offset excess methionine.
- **B-vitamin cofactors:** B1, B2, B6, B9 (folate), and B12 are all required to recycle methionine through the methylation cycle. Without them, homocysteine accumulates regardless of intake. Best food sources of B12 include beef liver, clams, sardines, and raw dairy.
- **Trimethylglycine (TMG/betaine):** Found naturally in beets, TMG donates methyl groups to homocysteine via a separate enzyme pathway (BHMT) and can be supplemented to support methionine recycling — particularly useful for those with MTHFR or MTR gene variants.

**Supplement forms** of methionine itself include free-form L-methionine powder or capsules. DL-methionine appears in some lipotropic formulations. Free-form amino acid supplements are well-absorbed without requiring digestion of a whole protein.

## Dosing

The US RDA for combined sulfur amino acids (methionine + cysteine) is **19 mg/kg/day** — roughly **1,330 mg/day** for a 70 kg adult — and is easily met through a mixed diet containing adequate protein. Supplementation at around **375–500 mg/day** is a common starting dose for those using standalone L-methionine products; up to **1,000 mg/day** of DL-methionine has been used in lipotropic formulations and is considered safe at that level. Higher doses are not generally supported for healthy adults and increase homocysteine risk.

For longevity or metabolic goals, *partial* methionine restriction — emphasizing plant proteins, collagen peptides, and glycine-rich foods within a total daily protein target of ~1.6 g/kg — is a practical strategy, rather than aggressive supplementation.

If using **TMG** to support the cycle, take it with a meal; cycle off every 2–3 weeks unless managing a confirmed elevated homocysteine, in which case limit use to 4–5 weeks at a time. **B12** is best taken early in the morning, away from large caffeine intake, and cycled unless you are clinically deficient.

## Risks

**Homocysteine toxicity** is the primary risk from excess methionine or impaired recycling. Homocysteine above 20 µmol/L is strongly associated with stroke, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, atherosclerosis, osteoporosis, and pregnancy complications. Target levels below 8–9 µmol/L. Elevated homocysteine signals a need to assess B6, B9, B12, zinc, and magnesium status — not simply to cut methionine.

**Excess methionine** without balancing glycine depletes B9 and B12, causes gut inflammation, inhibits thyroid function, and impairs mitochondrial integrity. Animal models show high-methionine diets promote obesity and accelerated aging.

**Genetic variants** in MTR (methionine synthase) or MTHFR can impair methionine recycling even with adequate dietary intake. Individuals suspecting undermethylation should also test ceruloplasmin and total copper, as free copper dysregulation commonly co-occurs.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **TMG supplements** interact with SAMe-based antidepressants and are problematic with alcohol or a compromised gut mucosal barrier.
- **B12 supplements** should not be combined with H2-receptor blockers or high caffeine intake, and can skew lab results — pause for one week before blood work.
- **Methionine supplementation broadly** can elevate homocysteine in those with impaired methylation pathways; baseline homocysteine testing before supplementing is advisable.
