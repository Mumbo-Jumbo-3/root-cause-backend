## What it's for

Magnesium participates in over 3,700 enzymatic reactions, making it one of the most foundational minerals in human physiology. Its core roles span virtually every major body system:

**Energy and mitochondria:** Required for ATP production — the energy currency of every cell. Mitochondria depend on magnesium to generate energy and manage redox signaling.

**Hormonal and metabolic:** Improves insulin sensitivity and glucose regulation, lowers SHBG (increasing free testosterone), and is necessary for the body to activate and utilize Vitamin D.

**Brain and nervous system:** Enhances GABA (the calming neurotransmitter), increases BDNF, supports neurogenesis, and has antidepressant mechanisms. Crucial for managing stress, anxiety, sleep quality, and addiction recovery.

**Cardiovascular:** Acts as an electrical conductor regulating the heartbeat, manages blood pressure, and supports active transport of calcium and potassium across cell membranes.

**Musculoskeletal:** Supports muscle relaxation, prevents cramps, aids nerve signaling, and is stored in large amounts in bone. Regulates calcium entry into cells.

**Detox, DNA, and repair:** Required for glutathione production (the body's master antioxidant), gene expression, DNA integrity, and every major detox and repair pathway.

**Gut and systemic:** Supports gut barrier integrity, liver health, migraine prevention, hearing protection, and sleep.

Deficiency is widespread due to soil depletion from industrial farming, high stress (which rapidly depletes magnesium), heavy exercise, poor dietary variety, and medications such as antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors, and diuretics. Common signs of deficiency include muscle cramps, poor sleep, anxiety, fatigue, constipation, brain fog, high blood pressure, and migraines.

## How to get it

**From food:** Prioritize magnesium-rich whole foods as a foundation. Top sources include cacao and dark chocolate (among the richest sources), leafy greens (spinach, Swiss chard), pumpkin seeds, almonds, cashews, legumes, whole grains, and fatty fish. Mineral water high in magnesium and bicarbonate is a practical daily source that also supports gut barrier health and reduces inflammation.

**Supplement forms — which to choose:**

Different forms of magnesium have different bioavailabilities and accumulate preferentially in different tissues. Using two or three forms across the day provides broader whole-body coverage than any single form alone.

- **Magnesium glycinate (bisglycinate):** The best general-purpose starting form for most people. Bound to glycine, it is well absorbed, gentle on the gut, calming, and has particular affinity for muscle tissue. Excellent for sleep, stress, and general repletion. Pairs powerfully with L-theanine for GABA support and brain waste clearance during sleep.
- **Magnesium chloride:** The body's default magnesium salt. Highly versatile — can be dissolved in drinking water for steady low-level saturation throughout the day, or applied topically as a spray or oil, bypassing the GI tract entirely. Approximately 25% elemental magnesium by weight.
- **Magnesium L-threonate:** Specifically designed to cross the blood-brain barrier. Best form for cognitive function, memory, and hearing protection. Best taken in the afternoon.
- **Magnesium taurate:** Taurine provides strong cardiovascular benefits. Excellent for anxiety, blood pressure, and heart health. A good morning form.
- **Magnesium malate:** Bound to malic acid (part of the citric acid/energy cycle). Well suited for muscle recovery, energy production, and detoxification. Preferred for chronic fatigue and fibromyalgia.
- **Magnesium sulfate (Epsom salts):** Best as a bath soak for transdermal muscle recovery. Not ideal for raising systemic magnesium levels orally.
- **Magnesium citrate:** Reserve for acute constipation only. The citrate component (commercially derived from Aspergillus mold fermentation) may negatively affect ceruloplasmin activity and should be avoided by those with MCAS or histamine intolerance.
- **Magnesium oxide:** Very low bioavailability. Generally not recommended.

**Routes of administration:**
1. Oral capsules or tablets — most common and well studied
2. Dissolved in water — magnesium chloride in drinking water provides gradual saturation with minimal GI burden
3. Topical spray or oil — magnesium chloride applied to skin; useful for those with digestive issues or high deficiency
4. Transdermal bath soak — Epsom salts or magnesium chloride flakes in bathwater
5. Ocean swimming — natural transdermal magnesium chloride exposure

Using multiple routes simultaneously supports the best whole-body tissue saturation.

**Timing:**
- Morning: Taurate or malate (energizing forms)
- Afternoon: L-threonate (cognitive support)
- Evening/night: Glycinate or chloride (sleep, relaxation, muscle recovery)

## Dosing

The official RDA for adults is 310–420 mg of elemental magnesium daily, but functional medicine practitioners widely consider this a floor, not an optimal target. For most adults, **400–600 mg of elemental magnesium per day** is a reasonable practical goal, achievable through a combination of food, supplements, and topical or dissolved forms.

**Elemental magnesium content varies by form:**
- Magnesium glycinate: approximately 10–14% elemental magnesium by weight (400 mg glycinate ≈ 40–56 mg elemental)
- Magnesium chloride: approximately 25% elemental magnesium by weight (1 g chloride ≈ 250 mg elemental)

**For beginners:** Start with 400 mg magnesium glycinate at night before bed. Optionally dissolve ¼ tsp magnesium chloride per liter of drinking water during the day. Monitor for bowel tolerance and increase gradually over weeks as needed.

**For more active, higher-demand, or higher-stress individuals:** Higher daily intakes across multiple forms and routes are appropriate. A practical multi-form split might include a taurate or malate form in the morning, L-threonate in the afternoon, and glycinate at night, while incorporating magnesium chloride in water throughout the day and topical application before exercise or morning sun exposure.

**Self-calibration with bowel tolerance:** Gradually increase your dose until you experience loose stools, then maintain just below that threshold — this represents your personal saturation ceiling. If you experience loose stools at very low doses, this may signal deficiencies in thiamine (B1), potassium, or sodium rather than true magnesium excess. In that case, switch to topical magnesium chloride, take oral forms with fiber, and address cofactor status before pushing oral doses higher.

## Risks

**GI effects:** The most common risk with oral supplementation is loose stools or diarrhea. This is self-limiting and not dangerous — simply reduce the dose or shift to topical application. It is very difficult to over-accumulate magnesium with healthy kidneys, as excess is excreted in urine.

**Kidney disease:** Those with impaired kidney function cannot clear magnesium efficiently and must exercise caution and consult a physician before supplementing.

**IV magnesium:** High-dose intravenous administration carries more serious risks and is a clinical concern only, not relevant to consumer supplementation.

**Upper limits:** The tolerable upper intake level for supplemental magnesium (not food-derived) is set at 350 mg/day by most regulatory bodies, above which GI effects become more likely. Higher doses are used therapeutically but should be approached gradually and with attention to bowel tolerance.

**Drug interactions:** Magnesium can meaningfully interfere with several classes of medications:
- **Antibiotics** — particularly tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones; magnesium chelates these drugs, significantly reducing their absorption. Separate dosing by at least 2 hours.
- **Blood pressure medications** — additive effects are possible; monitor blood pressure.
- **Diabetes medications** — magnesium improves insulin sensitivity; blood glucose effects may be altered.
- **Muscle relaxants** — magnesium has muscle-relaxing properties; combined effects may be enhanced.
- **Consult your physician before supplementing if you take any prescription medications.**

**Nutrient interactions to be aware of:**
- **Calcium:** Magnesium and calcium must be balanced. Excess calcium intake without adequate magnesium can be problematic, as magnesium regulates calcium entry into cells.
- **Vitamin D:** Magnesium is required to activate Vitamin D; taking them together is synergistic, but very high Vitamin D without adequate magnesium can accelerate magnesium depletion.
- **Thiamine (B1):** Required for proper magnesium uptake into mitochondria; B1 deficiency blocks magnesium utilization even when intake is adequate.
- **Zinc:** Supports the same hormonal optimization pathways; works synergistically with magnesium.
- **L-theanine:** Powerfully synergistic with glycinate for GABA production, sleep quality, and brain waste clearance.
