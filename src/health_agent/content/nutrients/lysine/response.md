## What it's for

Lysine is an essential amino acid the body cannot synthesize — it must come entirely from food or supplements. Its most critical structural role is in collagen formation: lysine residues are hydroxylated (with vitamin C as a cofactor) to create the cross-links that give skin, tendons, bones, blood vessels, and cartilage their tensile strength. Beyond structure, lysine competes with arginine for cellular uptake, which suppresses herpes simplex virus replication and reduces cold sore frequency and duration. It also serves as a precursor to carnitine (supporting mitochondrial fat oxidation), acts as a 5α-reductase type II inhibitor relevant to hair retention, and blocks serotonin receptors in the gut and adrenal glands — reducing gut inflammation, pain sensitivity, and cortisol output. Vegetarians and vegans are at elevated deficiency risk because most plant proteins, legumes excepted, are low in lysine.

## How to get it

**Food sources (minimally processed preferred)**

- Goat kefir and goat yogurt — among the highest-density sources, with probiotic co-benefits
- Milk and unripened (fresh) cheeses — high lysine with good bioavailability
- Other animal proteins (meat, fish, eggs) — reliably rich sources

**Heat processing caveat:** Heating lysine in the presence of sugars degrades it and can form carcinogenic compounds. Canned or powdered milk, toasted cereals, roasted nuts, and cooked soybeans all lose meaningful lysine content. Favor raw or lightly processed forms where practical.

**Supplement forms**

- **L-lysine HCl powder** — highest purity option; no capsule excipients, precise dosing, vegan-friendly (typically fermentation-derived), and water-soluble
- **L-lysine HCl capsules (clean-label)** — convenient for fixed daily doses; look for HPMC (vegetarian) capsules with no magnesium stearate, silicon dioxide, or fillers
- Take lysine **between meals** for optimal absorption; split higher daily totals across multiple doses rather than one large bolus

## Dosing

Daily needs vary significantly by goal:

- **Baseline maintenance / collagen support:** 1,000–2,000 mg/day, ideally paired with vitamin C
- **Cold sore prevention:** 1,000 mg/day; increase to 3,000 mg at symptom onset
- **Digestive / serotonin-modulating effects:** start at 200 mg/day and titrate up; the effective range for serotonin modulation is roughly 1–15 g/day
- **Hair growth (5α-reductase inhibition):** 6,000–16,000 mg/day proposed in some protocols
- **Immune recovery / high-demand states:** up to 16,000 mg/day has been used in recovery protocols

For most healthy adults, 1,000–3,000 mg/day covers the majority of supplemental use cases. Doses above 6,000 mg/day should be undertaken with physician supervision and, ideally, plasma amino acid monitoring. No formal Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) has been established.

## Risks

**High-dose caution:** No official UL exists, but gastrointestinal discomfort (nausea, cramping, diarrhea) becomes more likely as doses approach and exceed 10–15 g/day. Physician oversight and periodic lab monitoring are strongly recommended at those levels.

**Arginine competition:** Lysine and arginine share the same cellular transporters. High-dose lysine supplementation can suppress arginine-dependent nitric oxide synthesis — a relevant trade-off for athletes or individuals relying on arginine for cardiovascular vasodilation or performance.

**Estrogen interactions:** The enzymes involved in collagen cross-linking are also influenced by estrogen. Individuals on estrogen-based therapies should be aware that lysine metabolism may interact with those pathways, though the clinical magnitude is not well quantified.

**Lab work interference:** High-dose amino acid supplementation can skew blood test results. Discontinuing supplements for at least one week before bloodwork is a reasonable precaution.

**Heat + sugar reactions:** Dietary lysine heated in the presence of sugars (e.g., in processed soy products) can generate carcinogenic Maillard reaction byproducts — a reason to prefer minimally processed food sources and avoid heating lysine-rich foods with high-sugar ingredients unnecessarily.

**Drug interactions:** No well-documented direct drug–lysine interactions appear in the available evidence, but high-dose amino acid protocols should always be disclosed to a prescribing clinician, particularly for individuals on antivirals, hormonal therapies, or cardiovascular medications.
