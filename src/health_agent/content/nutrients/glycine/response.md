## What it's for

Glycine is the most abundant amino acid in collagen, occupying every third position in collagen's repeating structure, making it foundational to tendons, ligaments, cartilage, skin, the gut lining, and connective tissue throughout the body. Beyond structure, glycine acts as an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the spinal cord and brainstem — stabilizing nerve firing, supporting sleep architecture, and modulating circadian rhythms — while simultaneously serving as a co-agonist at NMDA receptors to support memory consolidation and learning. It is a direct precursor to glutathione (the body's master antioxidant), one of three amino acids required for creatine synthesis, a contributor to DNA and RNA via purine biosynthesis, and a suppressor of TNF-α–driven systemic inflammation. Modern diets built around boneless muscle meat provide far less glycine than traditional whole-animal diets, creating a chronically elevated methionine-to-glycine ratio that drives homocysteine accumulation, depleted glutathione, impaired methylation, and degraded connective tissue over time.

## How to get it

**Food sources** — the richest dietary glycine comes from collagen-dense animal tissues that most Western diets discard:

- **Bone broth** (long-simmered from bones, joints, skin, and feet): one of the most concentrated liquid sources
- **Gelatin** (denatured collagen from skin, bones, and hooves): highest-concentration solid food form
- **Oxtail, pig's feet, chicken feet, shanks**: extremely high in collagen-bound glycine
- **Chuck roast and other connective-tissue-rich cuts**: intermediate glycine content; meaningfully higher than lean cuts
- **Whole small fish and shellfish with skin/bones**: contribute skin collagen and shell matrix proteins
- **Organ meats**: more favorable glycine-to-methionine ratio than muscle meats

As a practical ratio, roughly 20 g of gelatin alongside a half-pound of steak helps counterbalance the methionine load of muscle meat.

**Supplement forms and trade-offs:**

- **Free-form glycine powder or capsules**: fastest absorption via neutral amino acid transporters; simplest and most direct
- **Hydrolyzed collagen peptides**: absorbed efficiently as intact di- and tripeptides (e.g., Gly-Pro-Hyp) via the PepT1 transporter; provides a broader amino acid matrix (proline, hydroxyproline) alongside glycine
- **Gelatin**: effective but requires more digestion than hydrolyzed collagen; useful for gut-coating effects
- **Mineral glycinates** (magnesium glycinate, zinc bisglycinate): deliver the glycine component while enhancing mineral bioavailability; not a primary glycine source
- **Sarcosine (methylglycine) and DMG (dimethylglycine)**: function as NMDA modulators or methyl donors respectively — not interchangeable with glycine for structural or collagen purposes

For collagen and connective tissue goals, hydrolyzed collagen peptides pair well with free-form glycine. For neurotransmitter, sleep, and metabolic benefits, free-form powder is the most direct route.

## Dosing

There is no official RDA for glycine, as it is classified conditionally non-essential. Endogenous synthesis provides roughly 3 g/day; typical Western diets add another 1.5–3 g/day — well below the estimated 10 g/day needed for optimal collagen turnover alone.

**Maintenance (healthy adults with some connective tissue intake):** 3–5 g/day supplemental glycine, taken in divided doses of ~1 g with meals.

**Repletion (minimal connective tissue intake, MTHFR variant, high glyphosate exposure, aging, or high methionine diet):** 10–15 g/day, distributed throughout the day rather than in large single doses.

**Specific uses:**
- *Sleep support:* 3 g taken 30–60 minutes before bed — well-supported in sleep quality research
- *Postprandial glucose blunting:* 3–5 g with carbohydrate-containing meals
- *Collagen synthesis:* 3–10 g/day glycine equivalent, ideally taken with vitamin C (required as cofactor for collagen hydroxylation)
- *Homocysteine/methylation support:* 5 g/day alongside adequate B6, B9, and B12

**Important timing note:** Doses of 5 g or more taken at night may disrupt REM sleep in many individuals. Keep the pre-bed dose to 3 g and distribute larger daily totals across daytime meals in ~1 g increments. High-demand states (severe deficiency, aging, significant toxic burden) may warrant up to 60 g/day, but plasma amino acid testing and physician supervision are strongly recommended above ~15 g/day.

## Risks

Glycine has a wide safety margin and no established Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL). Human studies have used single doses up to 60 g without acute toxicity. That said, several dose-dependent effects warrant attention.

**Side effects:** Higher doses can cause nausea, loose stools, or GI discomfort; distributing doses across the day minimizes this. As noted above, nighttime doses ≥5 g may disrupt REM sleep. Some individuals — particularly those low in chloride, magnesium, or GABA — experience a paradoxical excitatory response rather than the expected calming effect; correcting chloride/magnesium status or co-supplementing taurine, GABA, or L-theanine typically resolves this. Chronic high-dose use may blunt cognitive sharpness in some users, possibly via excessive inhibitory tone. Very high doses may cause transient hypotension consistent with glycine's blood-pressure-lowering mechanism.

**Safety cautions:** Those with impaired kidney or liver function should exercise caution at higher doses, as glycine metabolism depends on both organs.

**Drug interactions:**
- **Antipsychotics (including clozapine):** Glycine shares NMDA receptor modulation pathways with these drugs; high-dose glycine can alter drug efficacy and side-effect profiles — do not combine without physician oversight
- **SSRIs:** Glycine's anti-serotonin and TNF-α–suppressing actions may alter serotonergic drug pharmacodynamics; the interaction is explicitly flagged in the research

**Nutrient interactions:**
- **B vitamins (B1, B2, B6, B9, B12):** All required alongside glycine for complete methionine/homocysteine recycling; glycine alone is insufficient without these co-factors
- **Vitamin C:** Essential co-factor for collagen hydroxylation; co-supplementation is advisable when targeting connective tissue benefits
- **Taurine:** Shares bile acid conjugation and inhibitory neurotransmitter roles with glycine; balance the two rather than using either in isolation
- **Magnesium chloride/chloride:** Required for glycine's inhibitory chloride-current mechanism; deficiency predisposes to excitatory responses
- **Copper:** Modulates glycine demand; copper-deficient states increase requirements, as lysyl oxidase (copper-dependent) works downstream of glycine in collagen cross-linking
- **Creatine:** High-dose creatine supplementation reduces one avenue of glycine utilization (creatine synthesis), marginally lowering endogenous demand
