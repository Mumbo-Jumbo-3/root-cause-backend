## What it's for

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble essential vitamin that humans cannot produce and must obtain through food or supplements. It serves as one of the body's most versatile antioxidants, protecting DNA, proteins, and fats from oxidative damage, and it also regenerates vitamin E after it has neutralized free radicals.

Its best-established role is in **collagen synthesis**, making it indispensable for skin, blood vessels, bones, cartilage, and connective tissue integrity. The immune system relies on it heavily — white blood cells concentrate vitamin C at roughly a 60:1 ratio compared to surrounding fluids, and adequate levels strengthen the mucous membranes that form a first line of defense against infection.

Beyond these core roles, vitamin C supports cardiovascular health through endothelial function, nitric oxide activity, and blood pressure regulation; contributes to hormone and adrenal function, including modulating cortisol and supporting steroidogenesis; assists neurological and neurotransmitter metabolism (tyrosine pathway); aids bile flow; supports histamine clearance alongside B6 and copper; and acts as a chelating agent for heavy metals such as lead and cadmium, with roughly 1 g/day associated with meaningful reductions in blood levels of these metals.

Groups with elevated needs include smokers, people with high oxidative stress, those recovering from illness or chemotherapy, individuals with cardiovascular risk, and heavy alcohol users — all of whom deplete vitamin C significantly faster than average.

## How to get it

**Food first.** Whole food sources are generally preferred because they deliver vitamin C alongside complementary compounds. Top sources by concentration include:

- Acerola cherry and camu camu (exceptionally high)
- Wild berries, blackberries, strawberries, raspberries
- Kiwi, citrus juices, grapefruit juice, pomegranate juice (preferably without pulp for easier absorption)
- Pineapple, melon
- Organ meats such as grass-fed liver and spleen (an often-overlooked animal source)

**Supplement forms**, from highest to standard bioavailability:

- **Liposomal vitamin C** — encapsulated in phospholipid bilayers, delivering significantly better cellular uptake with less gastrointestinal upset; the most effective oral form
- **Sodium ascorbate** — buffered, less acidic, and gentler on the stomach than plain ascorbic acid
- **Ascorbic acid** — the standard form; effective and widely available; best taken with food to reduce acidity
- **Whole-food C complexes with bioflavonoids** — may offer added synergistic benefit
- **IV vitamin C** — highest bioavailability; used clinically but not practical for routine home use

**Absorption tips:** Take supplemental ascorbic acid with a meal containing sodium, and wash it down with an acidic beverage such as citrus juice to optimize uptake and reduce gastric discomfort. Avoid taking large doses alongside iron-rich foods (see Risks). Time supplements in the morning or 1–2 hours after caffeine intake.

**Beneficial pairings:** Copper (essential at high doses — see Risks), zinc (30–60 mg/day for immune protocols), vitamin E (synergistic antioxidant partnership), B6 (histamine clearance), and NAC (combined antioxidant and liver support).

## Dosing

Vitamin C absorption follows a saturation curve. Up to approximately 200 mg/day, blood and tissue levels rise efficiently and the kidneys actively reabsorb it. Beyond that point, retention efficiency decreases, though at 5 g/day total body retention is still roughly four times that of 100 mg/day.

**Baseline intake from food** should ideally cover the foundational 75–90 mg daily minimum through the dietary sources above. For most people eating a varied diet rich in fruit, this is achievable without supplementation.

**When supplementing**, a practical starting point is **250 mg/day**. A moderate therapeutic range of **500 mg – 2 g/day in split doses** covers most health-maintenance goals, including immune support, collagen production, and antioxidant protection. Higher intakes in the **1–2 g/day** range are used for specific purposes such as heavy metal chelation, smoking-related depletion, or cardiovascular risk reduction.

**Split dosing is strongly preferred** over single large doses. Because vitamin C is water-soluble and cannot be stored in meaningful quantities, spreading doses across two to three times per day maintains more consistent plasma levels throughout the day.

Continuous supplementation beyond **2–3 months** at higher doses warrants caution, primarily due to cumulative copper depletion (see Risks). Cycling — taking periodic breaks — is a sensible approach for long-term use.

## Risks

**Copper depletion** is the most clinically significant risk of sustained high-dose vitamin C. Large doses lower copper status over time, which is likely responsible for some of the negative outcomes seen in long-term high-dose studies. Do not use high-dose vitamin C if your copper status is already low, and ensure adequate dietary or supplemental copper when taking more than 1 g/day for extended periods.

**Kidney stones:** High doses, particularly of ascorbic acid, can increase oxalate excretion and raise kidney stone risk in susceptible individuals. Those with a history of oxalate stones should exercise caution and consult a clinician before exceeding 1 g/day.

**GI distress:** Doses that exceed an individual's "bowel tolerance" threshold cause diarrhea, nausea, and cramping. This threshold varies widely — some people tolerate 1 g; others tolerate much more. Liposomal and buffered (sodium ascorbate) forms tend to be better tolerated at higher doses.

**Upper limits and cycling:** Continuous use at high doses beyond 2–3 months may produce adverse effects, most attributable to copper depletion rather than vitamin C toxicity directly. Cycling is recommended for ongoing high-dose use.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**

- **Iron-rich foods or supplements** — do not combine large doses of vitamin C at the same meal; ascorbate significantly enhances iron absorption and can amplify oxidative stress when unbound iron is present
- **Aspirin** — reduces vitamin C absorption and increases urinary excretion
- **Anticoagulants (blood thinners)** — potential interaction; consult a clinician
- **Statins** — may interfere with metabolism
- **Hormonal contraceptives (birth control pills)** — estrogen medications can affect vitamin C levels and vice versa
- **Certain antibiotics** — separate timing is recommended
- **Supplemental B vitamins** — avoid combining in the same meal at high doses due to competition or interaction effects
