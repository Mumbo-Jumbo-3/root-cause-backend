## What it's for

Carnitine's primary role is shuttling long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria for beta-oxidation and ATP production, making it foundational to cellular energy metabolism. Beyond energy, it reduces lactic acid buildup, supports androgen receptor sensitivity (improving testosterone uptake), enhances male fertility, and offers cognitive benefits — particularly in acetylated form. It also plays a protective role against catecholamine-induced mitochondrial damage and assists in nitrogen metabolism by helping lower ammonia levels. Deficiency manifests as fatigue, poor exercise tolerance, brain fog, difficulty losing body fat, and reduced muscle anabolism — carnitine contributes to muscle protein synthesis and helps prevent muscle wasting alongside nutrients like leucine and creatine.

## How to get it

**Food sources**

Carnitine is concentrated almost exclusively in animal foods. Top dietary sources include:
- Red meats: beef, lamb, bison, venison (highest concentrations)
- Dairy products (moderate amounts)
- Plant foods contribute negligibly

**Supplement forms — choose by goal**

- **L-carnitine / L-carnitine tartrate** — general energy, fat metabolism, androgen receptor support, and exercise recovery; tartrate form is stable and well-studied
- **Acetyl-L-carnitine (ALCAR)** — preferred for cognitive and neurological support; crosses the blood-brain barrier effectively
- **Injectable L-carnitine** — used clinically; substantially higher bioavailability than oral forms

**Bioavailability note**

Oral absorption from food is only 5–15%, and oral supplements share similar limitations. Injectable forms bypass this but require clinical supervision. Taking oral carnitine with fat-containing meals improves absorption.

## Dosing

No RDA exists for healthy adults, as the body synthesizes carnitine endogenously. Supplemental dosing is context-dependent:

- **General maintenance / performance:** 1.5–3 g/day, split into two doses, taken with fat-containing meals; pre-workout timing is optimal for exercise contexts
- **Higher-demand repletion:** up to 4 g/day in divided doses
- **Elderly populations:** 2 g/day orally is a well-studied benchmark — centenarian research showed this dose reduced fat mass, increased lean mass, and improved both physical and mental fatigue
- **Form-specific note:** ALCAR doses (typically 500 mg–2 g/day) are used for cognitive goals; L-carnitine tartrate is preferred for metabolic and exercise applications

**Cycling:** Limit continuous use to no more than 2 months at a time due to evidence of long-term thyroid effects with prolonged supplementation.

## Risks

**TMAO and cardiovascular risk** — The most significant concern. Oral carnitine can raise TMAO many-fold, a metabolite associated with atherosclerosis, reduced kidney function, and increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. In inflammatory states such as sepsis, it can activate the pyrin inflammasome via OCTN2, worsening outcomes through IL-1β and IL-17 release. High doses also cause fishy body odor from gut bacterial conversion of unabsorbed carnitine to trimethylamine (TMA); combining with aged garlic extract may partially inhibit TMAO formation.

**Oxidative stress** — There is evidence that carnitine supplementation increases oxidative damage in individuals with a high burden of unsaturated fats, warranting caution in those with high PUFA intake.

**Thyroid effects** — Long-term use is associated with thyroid suppression; the 2-month cycling limit is intended to mitigate this.

**Drug interactions** — L-carnitine interacts with anticoagulants (warfarin, acenocoumarol) and thyroid medications; those on these drugs should consult a clinician before supplementing.

No official tolerable upper limit (UL) has been formally established, but the TMAO and thyroid evidence argues for conservative, cycled use — especially in those with cardiovascular risk factors or pre-existing inflammation.
