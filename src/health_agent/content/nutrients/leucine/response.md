## What it's for

Leucine is an essential branched-chain amino acid that serves as the most potent activator of mTORC1, the primary molecular switch for muscle protein synthesis. It works by binding Sestrin2 to release the GATOR2 complex, which removes the brake on mTORC1 and triggers anabolic signaling. Beyond muscle, leucine stimulates insulin release, supports bone and wound healing, influences hunger and brain energy metabolism, and feeds ketone body synthesis (it is strictly ketogenic, not glucogenic). It also functions as a nutrient-sensing signal — telling the body that sufficient protein is available — making it central not just to building muscle but to overall metabolic regulation, including how the body responds to protein intake at each meal.

## How to get it

**Whole-food sources** are the most bioavailable and context-rich delivery vehicle:
- Grass-fed beef, pasture-raised eggs, raw milk, ricotta and cottage cheese, chicken, and whey-based foods are all reliable high-leucine options
- Animal proteins (whey, eggs, beef) have superior bioavailability compared to most plant proteins, making them more efficient per gram

**Supplement forms — key distinctions:**
- **Free-form L-leucine powder:** Rapid absorption spike via shared neutral amino acid transporters (SLC6A19); practical for hitting the anabolic threshold quickly, but competes with other neutral amino acids at uptake
- **Peptide-bound leucine (hydrolyzed whey isolate):** Absorbed via the PepT1 transporter with the full essential amino acid (EAA) profile intact, offering more sustained anabolic signaling
- **Fermented L-leucine** (used by NutraBio, ALLMAX): Vegan-sourced and considered a clean production method

**Practical tip:** Adequate sodium supports leucine absorption through the sodium-dependent SLC6A19 transporter — often overlooked. Balancing leucine-heavy animal protein with some glycine (e.g., from collagen-rich foods or gelatin) helps offset methionine-skewed methylation from high meat intake.

## Dosing

**Baseline daily target:** The historical RDA is 42 mg/kg/day, but evidence now supports 78 mg/kg/day for older adults. For a 75 kg person, that translates to roughly 3.2 g/day at the RDA, and ~5.9 g/day at the updated older-adult figure — spread across meals rather than taken all at once.

**Per-meal threshold:** The critical practical number is **2.5–3 g of leucine per meal** to meaningfully trigger muscle protein synthesis. Below this threshold, a meal's protein may be largely anabolically inert. This typically requires ~25–30 g of high-quality complete protein per meal from sources like beef, eggs, or whey.

**Supplemental use:** Free-form L-leucine supplements are typically dosed at 2–5 g per sitting, timed around training or meals that might otherwise fall short of the anabolic threshold. Pair leucine-containing protein meals with some carbohydrate — large protein loads without carbs can cause reactive blood sugar drops that trigger cortisol and adrenaline release, promoting insulin resistance over time.

**Higher-demand groups:** Older adults and athletes in repletion phases should prioritize clearing the 2.5–3 g per-meal leucine threshold at every main meal.

## Risks

**No formal upper limit (UL) has been established** by regulatory bodies for leucine, but several caution signals exist at high sustained intake:

- **Ammonia accumulation:** Excess isolated leucine elevates ammonia, contributing to brain fog. This is why balanced full-spectrum amino acid intake (not leucine alone) is preferable for daily use
- **Cardiovascular signals:** High BCAA intake, including leucine, has been associated with increased heart disease risk in some research — the mechanism is not fully resolved but warrants moderation in long-term high-dose protocols
- **Serotonin and neurotransmitter interference:** Very high leucine can disrupt serotonin signaling. Isolated high-dose leucine for serotonin modulation is considered less effective and riskier than alternatives like glycine, ginger, or thiamine
- **Blood sugar cascade:** High protein without carbohydrate co-ingestion can sharply drop blood glucose, triggering adrenaline and cortisol release, lipolysis, and downstream insulin resistance — a risk amplified by leucine's own insulin-stimulating effect
- **Drug and lab interactions:** Amino acid supplementation broadly can skew lab results (e.g., metabolic panels); discontinuing supplementation for one week before blood work is advisable. Leucine's insulin-stimulating effect is relevant for anyone on hypoglycemic medications — monitor blood sugar accordingly
- **Methionine imbalance:** Heavy reliance on leucine-dense animal proteins without glycine-rich foods can skew methylation; balance with glycine from collagen, gelatin, or bone broth
