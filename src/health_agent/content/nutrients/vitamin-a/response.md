## What it's for

Vitamin A is an essential fat-soluble micronutrient your body cannot produce on its own — it must come entirely from diet. It is stored in the liver and serves a remarkably wide range of physiological roles:

**Vision:** Supports night vision and light adaptation, maintains corneal and conjunctival health, and supports retinal function. Night blindness is among the earliest and most classic signs of deficiency.

**Skin and surface tissue integrity:** Drives daily turnover of skin cells and maintains the mucosal linings of the respiratory tract, gut, bladder, and eyes — forming barrier defenses against infection. Dry eyes, rough or bumpy skin, and poor wound healing are common deficiency signals.

**Immune function:** Maintains and strengthens defenses against infections (including fungal), stimulates interleukin-2 production, and modulates suppressor cell activity in a hormone-like fashion.

**Hormonal and reproductive health:** Critical for fertility in both sexes. Supports synthesis and regulation of pregnenolone, DHEA, progesterone, testosterone, LH, and FSH. Functions as an estrogen antagonist in various cell types, inhibiting excessive cell division.

**Metabolic and developmental roles:** Promotes protein synthesis, assists absorption of protein, calcium, and iodine, supports thyroid function, and is essential for preventing birth defects during pregnancy. Also protects against lipid peroxidation and regulates lysosomal cell function.

## How to get it

Vitamin A exists in two dietary forms:

- **Preformed retinol** — found in animal foods, immediately bioavailable
- **Provitamin A carotenoids (e.g., beta-carotene)** — found in plants, must be converted to retinol in the body

**Best animal-based sources (highest bioavailability):**
- Beef, duck, chicken, lamb, or turkey **liver** — the single richest and most reliable source
- Other organ meats (heart, kidney)
- Pasture-raised or free-range **egg yolks**
- Raw and aged **cheese**
- **Grass-fed butter and ghee**
- Clams
- Red palm oil

**Plant-based carotenoid sources (supportive, not primary):**
- Pumpkin, squash, sweet potatoes, carrots
- Cantaloupe, mango, apricots, peaches
- Dark leafy greens

> **Important:** Beta-carotene conversion to retinol is inefficient in many people due to genetic variation (BCMO1 polymorphisms), hypothyroidism, fat malabsorption, or poor gut health. Plant sources alone are insufficient as a sole strategy for many individuals.

**A practical food pattern for optimal intake:**
- Liver or organ meats 2–3 times per week (~100–150g per serving)
- Free-range eggs ~4 times per week
- Raw or fermented dairy and grass-fed butter daily if tolerated
- Always consume vitamin A–rich foods **with dietary fat** — as a fat-soluble vitamin, absorption depends on it
- When eating liver frequently, pair it with **sulfur-rich foods** (garlic, onions) to help moderate iron co-absorption

**Key cofactors that support vitamin A function:**
- **Vitamin E** — protects vitamin A from oxidative destruction; the two should always be paired, especially with supplements
- **Vitamin D** — works synergistically for proper vitamin A utilization and gene expression
- **Vitamin K2** — works in concert for skin, bone, and cellular health
- **Zinc** — required for retinol-binding protein synthesis; zinc deficiency impairs vitamin A transport
- **Thyroid hormones** — support efficient vitamin A metabolism
- **Dietary fat** — essential for absorption at every meal containing vitamin A

**On supplements:** Because vitamin A is fat-soluble and accumulates in the liver, food sources are strongly preferred for most people. Supplements carry a meaningfully higher toxicity risk than whole foods, which come naturally buffered with cofactors. If supplements are used, they should always be paired with vitamin E (200–400 IU d-alpha tocopherol) to reduce oxidative degradation and toxicity risk.

## Dosing

**Baseline from food (adequate intake for most healthy adults):**
The standard recommended daily intake is approximately **700–900 mcg RAE** (roughly 2,300–3,000 IU) per day from food. A 100–150g serving of beef liver two to three times per week, combined with regular eggs, full-fat dairy, and grass-fed butter, reliably meets and often exceeds this target without any risk of toxicity.

**When supplementation applies:**
Supplements are appropriate in cases of confirmed deficiency, poor diet diversity, impaired absorption, or clinical therapeutic contexts — not for general routine use. A typical supplemental dose of **2,500–10,000 IU (750–3,000 mcg RAE)** as retinyl palmitate covers most corrective needs in otherwise healthy adults. Higher doses (up to 25,000 IU) are sometimes used short-term in clinical settings for repletion under supervision. Whatever the dose, vitamin E should always accompany supplemental vitamin A.

**Pregnancy:** Do not exceed **10,000 IU/day** from supplements during pregnancy — higher doses are teratogenic. Food-based retinol remains the safest approach during pregnancy. Discuss all supplementation with a clinician.

## Risks

**Toxicity (hypervitaminosis A):**
Because vitamin A is stored in the liver, it can accumulate. Chronic excess — far more likely from supplements than food — causes headache, nausea, bone pain, hair loss, skin peeling, liver damage, and increased intracranial pressure. Both deficiency *and* excess follow a U-shaped risk curve: too little and too much are both harmful.

**Bone health:** Chronically excessive vitamin A from supplements may paradoxically contribute to bone loss by interfering with vitamin D and K2 activity and impairing thyroid function.

**Autoimmunity:** Overdosing may worsen autoimmune conditions.

**Upper tolerable limit:** The established upper limit for adults is **3,000 mcg RAE (10,000 IU) per day** from preformed retinol. Staying below this level from supplements is important for long-term safety; food-based intake is self-limiting and not associated with toxicity under normal conditions.

**Pregnancy:** Supplemental vitamin A above ~10,000 IU/day is teratogenic. Pregnant individuals should prioritize food sources and consult a clinician before supplementing.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **Vitamin E deficiency** accelerates oxidative destruction of vitamin A — always pair the two
- **High PUFA (polyunsaturated fat) diets** may deplete both vitamin A and vitamin E by increasing lipid peroxidation
- **Retinoid medications** (isotretinoin, tretinoin, acitretin): combining with supplemental vitamin A creates serious toxicity risk — avoid supplementation while on these drugs without medical supervision
- **Orlistat** (fat-blocking weight loss drug) reduces absorption of all fat-soluble vitamins, including vitamin A
- **Cholestyramine and other bile acid sequestrants** similarly impair fat-soluble vitamin absorption
- **Zinc deficiency** impairs vitamin A transport by reducing retinol-binding protein synthesis — correct zinc status first if both are low
- **Warfarin (blood thinners):** High-dose vitamin A may interact with anticoagulant therapy — consult a clinician
