## What it's for

Vitamin A is an essential fat-soluble vitamin that supports multiple critical body functions. It's primarily known for vision support, helping with light sensitivity and proper eye function. The vitamin maintains immune system strength by supporting the integrity of surface tissues including skin, respiratory tract, gut lining, bladder, and inner ear while helping fight infections.

Beyond these core functions, vitamin A is crucial for hormone production, particularly pregnenolone, progesterone, testosterone, and other youth-associated steroids. It regulates cell division, promotes protein synthesis, and acts as an antioxidant. The vitamin is essential for reproductive health and fertility, prevents birth defects, and enhances thyroid function by improving iodine absorption and hormone production.

Research indicates vitamin A also acts as a potent gut anti-inflammatory that may reverse colon damage and provide therapeutic value for conditions like IBD. It supports mitochondrial energy production and contributes to brain development and skin health.

## How to get it

The most bioavailable vitamin A comes from animal sources containing preformed vitamin A (retinol). The best food sources include liver (beef, duck, chicken, lamb, turkey) which has the highest concentration, other organ meats, raw cheese, egg yolks, grass-fed butter and ghee, and clams and other shellfish. These sources are far superior to plant sources like carrots and leafy greens, which contain beta-carotene that must be converted to retinol—a process that's often inefficient in many people.

When choosing supplements, prioritize those containing preformed retinol (retinyl palmitate) or cod liver oil sources over beta-carotene for superior bioavailability. Look for products that avoid unnecessary fillers, binders, artificial colors, and common contaminants. Always combine vitamin A supplementation with vitamin E (200-400 IU) to prevent oxidative damage, as vitamin A should never be taken alone in supplement form.

## Dosing

For general health maintenance, prioritize getting vitamin A from food sources through regular consumption of liver weekly and other animal sources daily. When supplementation is necessary, typical doses range from 10,000 IU for maintenance to 25,000 IU daily for inflammatory conditions, though therapeutic doses of 50,000-100,000 IU have been used under medical supervision.

Always combine supplemental vitamin A with vitamin E to prevent oxidative damage. Since vitamin A is stored in the liver, consistent moderate intake is preferable to large intermittent doses. The specific amount needed varies based on individual factors including thyroid function, light exposure, and overall oxidative stress levels.

## Risks

Synthetic vitamin A supplements can cause significant toxicity including liver damage, bone pain, hair loss, dry skin, neurological symptoms (irritability, confusion), and interference with vitamin K and D metabolism. High doses may also inhibit thyroid function, particularly if thyroid status is suboptimal.

Pregnant women should be particularly cautious with high doses due to birth defect risks. Vitamin A can interact problematically with unsaturated fats, which can destroy protective vitamin E, and may interfere with vitamin K-dependent clotting factors at high doses. However, when combined with protective cofactors like vitamin E, thyroid hormones, vitamin D, and progesterone, vitamin A utilization improves and toxicity risk decreases. Always ensure adequate thyroid hormone status to reduce toxicity risk.
