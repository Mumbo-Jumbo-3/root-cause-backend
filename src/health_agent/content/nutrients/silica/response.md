## What it's for

Silica (silicon dioxide, SiO₂) is a trace mineral that the body uses in its bioavailable form — **orthosilicic acid (OSA)** — for several important functions:

- **Connective tissue and collagen synthesis:** Silicon concentrates in skin, tendons, ligaments, cartilage, and bone matrix, where it supports collagen and elastin cross-linking. Adequate intake is associated with better skin elasticity, hair strength, nail integrity, and joint health.
- **Bone mineralization:** Silicon participates in early bone calcification alongside calcium and phosphorus. Higher dietary silicon intake has been linked to greater bone mineral density, particularly in premenopausal women.
- **Aluminum detoxification and neuroprotection:** Silicon competes with aluminum for absorption in the gut and can mobilize aluminum from tissues — including the brain — for urinary excretion. Studies suggest silica-rich mineral water may reduce aluminum bioavailability by up to 60–70%. Epidemiological data associates higher dietary silicon intake (~10 mg/day from drinking water) with reduced risk of cognitive decline, though this remains an active area of research rather than established clinical consensus.
- **Cardiovascular health:** Silicon is present in arterial walls and may help maintain their elasticity, potentially countering age-related arterial stiffening.

Silicon is considered a conditionally essential trace element. No formal RDA has been established in most countries.

## How to get it

**Food and drink sources (preferred, highest bioavailability first):**

- **High-silica mineral water** — the most bioavailable form, as silicon exists as OSA in water and is 50–85% absorbed. Fiji Water (~93 mg/L) and Volvic (~20 mg/L) are well-known examples; drinking ~1 liter daily provides a meaningful dose.
- **Nettle leaf tea** — a gentle, reliable source suitable for regular use several times per week.
- **Horsetail (Equisetum arvense) tea or extract** — exceptionally high in silicon. Use 2–3 times per week only; do not use daily or continuously (see Risks).
- **Oats and whole grains** — among the highest silicon foods by weight, though bioavailability is lower than liquid sources.
- **Bee pollen (glyphosate-free)** — a concentrated source of silica and other trace minerals; mix 1:1 with raw honey if digestive sensitivity is a concern.
- **Bananas, green beans, and lentils** — moderate sources.
- **Shilajit (verified heavy-metal-free)** — contains fulvic acid and trace silicon; quality verification is essential.
- **Beer (from hops and malted barley)** — surprisingly high in bioavailable silicon (~6–12 mg per serving), though not appropriate in all contexts.

**Supplemental forms:**

- **Orthosilicic acid (OSA)** — the most bioavailable supplemental form (e.g., ch-OSA/BioSil). Studied in clinical trials for skin, hair, and nail benefits.
- **Colloidal silica gel** — well-absorbed liquid form used widely in Europe for connective tissue support.
- **Monomethylsilanetriol (MMST)** — a stabilized, highly bioavailable liquid form derived from mineral or plant sources.
- **Horsetail extract** — widely available but requires careful cycling (see Dosing and Risks).

**Critical form to avoid — particulate/fumed silicon dioxide (E551):** This industrial additive used as a flow agent or anti-caking agent in tablets, capsules, and processed foods is *not* the same as bioavailable dietary silicon. It can activate intestinal macrophages, disrupt gut barrier function, and has documented inflammatory and potentially genotoxic properties. When purchasing any supplement — not just silica — check ingredient labels and avoid products listing silicon dioxide (E551) as an excipient.

## Dosing

No official RDA exists for silicon, but research and population data provide useful reference points. Typical Western dietary intake is estimated at **20–50 mg/day** from food. The level associated with epidemiological benefit for aluminum reduction and cognitive health is approximately **10 mg/day from drinking water alone**, achievable by consuming ~1 liter of high-silica mineral water daily.

For supplemental orthosilicic acid (OSA), clinical studies have used **5–10 mg of elemental silicon per day** — a modest dose consistent with food-level intake, not a megadose. Horsetail-based protocols often reference 1–3 g of extract daily, but elemental silicon content varies significantly by product; use manufacturer-specified silicon content rather than extract weight as your guide.

The practical approach: prioritize silica-rich mineral water and dietary sources as your baseline. If food sources are insufficient or you are targeting a specific goal (e.g., connective tissue support, aluminum clearance), add an OSA or colloidal silica supplement at 5–10 mg elemental silicon per day. Cycle horsetail-based products — use no more than 2–3 times per week, and take a break after 4 continuous weeks of use.

## Risks

**Side effects and upper limits:**
- No formal tolerable upper intake level (UL) has been established for dietary silicon. At food and mineral water doses, silicon is well tolerated.
- Very high supplemental doses are not well characterized in humans; a precautionary approach is warranted.
- **Horsetail specifically** contains thiaminase, an enzyme that degrades vitamin B1 (thiamine). Long-term or daily use can deplete thiamine. Horsetail may also affect DHT levels at higher or continuous doses. Restrict use to 2–3 times per week and cycle off after 4 weeks.
- **Fumed/nanoparticulate silicon dioxide (E551)** — as noted above, this additive form carries documented inflammatory, gut-barrier-disrupting, and potentially genotoxic risks distinct from dietary silicon. Avoid it as a supplement or food additive wherever possible.
- **Crystalline silica** (from occupational dust inhalation) is a known carcinogen; this is not relevant to dietary or supplemental intake but is worth distinguishing.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **Aluminum:** Silicon actively competes with aluminum for GI absorption and promotes its excretion — a beneficial interaction when aluminum burden is a concern. By clearing aluminum, silica indirectly supports calcium and magnesium absorption, which aluminum impairs.
- **H2 blockers (e.g., famotidine, ranitidine):** May reduce silica absorption; avoid pairing these medications with silica supplementation if possible.
- **Caffeine (large amounts):** May reduce silicon absorption; avoid taking silica supplements alongside high-caffeine intake.
- **Hormonal considerations:** Horsetail at higher or continuous doses has been noted to potentially reduce DHT; individuals sensitive to hormonal changes should use it conservatively.
- **Heavy metals broadly:** Silicon and boron may support heavy metal excretion generally, which can be a consideration if you are on chelation protocols — consult a clinician.
- **Shilajit:** Must be independently verified as heavy-metal-free before use, as contamination is a known issue with many commercial products.
