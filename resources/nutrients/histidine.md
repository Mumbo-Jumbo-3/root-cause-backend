# Histidine

Aliases: L-histidine

## What it's for

Histidine is a semi-essential amino acid best known as the direct precursor to histamine, a biogenic amine with sweeping influence across the body. Through the enzyme histidine decarboxylase (HDC), histidine is converted to histamine in mast cells, basophils, gastric cells, and hypothalamic neurons — regulating gastric acid secretion, immune function (T cells, B cells, dendritic cells), and neurotransmission. In the skin, histidine supports UV protection and moisture retention. It also serves as a building block for carnosine (alongside beta-alanine), a dipeptide with muscle-buffering, anxiolytic, and dopamine-sparing effects relevant to physical and cognitive performance. Because histamine is a potent inflammatory mediator, there is emerging interest in whether modest dietary histidine restriction could have longevity-promoting effects, similar to arguments made for tryptophan and cysteine.

## How to get it

**Food sources:**
- Animal proteins (meat, poultry, fish, eggs) are the richest dietary sources of histidine
- Fresh, high-quality seafood is preferable — bacteria in spoiled fish convert histidine to histamine, causing scombroid poisoning
- Gelatin and collagen contain only small amounts of histidine, making them useful for those intentionally moderating histamine-driving amino acids
- Fermented foods (aged cheese, wine, natto, kombucha) already contain preformed histamine from bacterial conversion; those with impaired histamine clearance should limit these

**Dairy form matters:** A1 dairy contains histidine at position 67 of the beta-casein chain, which cleaves to release BCM-7 and can raise histamine, promoting inflammation and allergy-like reactions. A2 dairy has proline at that position instead and is the better choice for histamine-sensitive individuals.

**Supplement forms:**
- **Free-form L-histidine powder or capsules** — the standard supplemental form; well-absorbed as a free amino acid
- **Beta-alanine** — the preferred ergogenic route for raising muscle carnosine; histidine is rarely the rate-limiting substrate, so supplementing beta-alanine (3.2–6.4 g/day) is more efficient than supplementing histidine directly for this purpose
- **DAO enzyme supplements** — support histamine breakdown before high-histamine meals; useful for those with impaired clearance
- **Cofactor nutrients** — vitamin C, copper, zinc, B6 (as P5P), B9, B12, and magnesium all support either HDC, DAO, or HNMT enzyme activity and are integral to healthy histidine metabolism

## Dosing

Adults require approximately **8–12 mg/kg/day** of histidine from food; for a 70 kg person, that is roughly **560–840 mg/day**. The US RDA is **14 mg/kg/day (~980 mg)**. A varied diet with quality animal protein typically meets this without supplementation.

For those who supplement directly, research doses of **500–2,000 mg/day** of L-histidine are common, taken with meals to support absorption alongside other amino acids. Clinical caution is advised above **4,000 mg/day** based on available human and animal data, as excess histidine drives histamine production. In most repletion scenarios — histamine intolerance, mast cell issues — the more effective strategy is correcting cofactor deficiencies (B6, B12, C, copper, zinc, magnesium) and supporting DAO/HNMT enzyme function rather than manipulating histidine intake directly. For muscle carnosine loading, dose **beta-alanine at 3.2–6.4 g/day** in divided doses rather than supplementing histidine.

## Risks

**Excess histamine:** Because supplemental histidine converts directly to histamine, high doses in individuals with compromised DAO or HNMT function can cause histamine accumulation — manifesting as headaches, migraines, anxiety, gut disturbance, asthma flares, skin reactions, and sleep apnea. There is no established Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL), but caution is warranted above 4,000 mg/day.

**Drug and nutrient interactions:**
- **DAO-inhibiting substances** — excess alcohol, coffee, and certain medications (some antihistamines, NSAIDs, antidepressants) reduce histamine clearance, amplifying the load from dietary or supplemental histidine
- **Mycotoxins** (ochratoxin A in cheap commercial cereals and coffee) specifically inhibit DAO activity, worsening histamine accumulation
- **High-dose P5P (active B6)** could theoretically increase histamine synthesis by enhancing HDC activity — relevant when co-supplementing B6 with histidine
- **Zinc status** modulates mast cell histamine release directly; deficiency worsens histamine excess
- **Antihistamine drug overuse** to manage histidine-driven histamine load can cause secondary problems: SIBO, Candida overgrowth, and constipation via muscarinic receptor blockade in the gut

Individuals with mast cell activation syndrome, histamine intolerance, or known DAO/HNMT gene variants (SNPs) should consult a clinician before supplementing histidine.
