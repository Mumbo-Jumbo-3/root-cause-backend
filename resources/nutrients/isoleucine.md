# Isoleucine

Aliases: L-isoleucine

## What it's for

Isoleucine is an essential branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) with broad physiological roles including wound healing, blood sugar regulation, immune function, and hormone production. Unlike its BCAA sibling leucine, isoleucine is both glucogenic and ketogenic, meaning it can feed into both glucose and fat synthesis pathways — giving it unusual metabolic versatility. It fuels oxidative energy metabolism directly through the mitochondrial TCA cycle, relying on thiamine- and biotin-dependent enzymes to complete that process. Notably, animal research shows that restricting isoleucine can extend healthspan and lifespan by roughly 33% via mTOR pathway modulation, signaling that chronic excess carries meaningful downsides rather than added benefit.

## How to get it

Because isoleucine is essential, it must come entirely from diet or supplements — the body cannot synthesize it.

**Preferred whole-food sources** (listed in rough order of density and co-factor richness):
- Grass-fed beef and raw/grass-fed dairy (also supply the B-vitamins — thiamine and biotin — needed to metabolize isoleucine)
- Pasture-raised eggs and ricotta cheese
- Whey protein (a practical whole-protein option around training)
- AFA algae (*Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*), which supplies all essential amino acids alongside vitamins and minerals

**Supplement forms:**
- **Free-form L-isoleucine powder** — fastest absorption, useful for precise dosing or building custom BCAA blends
- **Whole-protein powders** (whey, beef isolate) — isoleucine arrives alongside co-factors and complementary amino acids; generally preferred over isolated forms
- **BCAA blends** — common but often contain fillers; check labels carefully

**Bioavailability note:** Animal-sourced whole foods naturally co-deliver the thiamine and biotin that isoleucine catabolism requires. When using concentrated powders on a high-protein diet, intentionally ensuring adequate B-vitamin intake becomes more important.

## Dosing

The WHO/FAO Estimated Average Requirement for isoleucine is approximately **20 mg per kilogram of body weight per day** — roughly **1,400 mg/day** for a 70 kg adult. A standard omnivorous diet providing 1.5–2 g of protein per kilogram of body weight generally meets this without supplementation.

**Supplemental use:** Isolated isoleucine powder products suggest serving sizes around **500 mg–1,000 mg**, typically used to fortify custom BCAA blends rather than as standalone high-dose supplements. No formal repletion protocol for isolated isoleucine exists in the literature; needs should be met through food first.

**Training context:** Exercise and dehydration can reduce circulating BCAA levels, making the post-workout window a practical time to consume isoleucine-containing whole proteins (whey, beef). High protein intake also increases demand for biotin and thiamine as co-factors — ensure these are adequate rather than simply increasing isoleucine dose further.

The longevity data and ammonia-accumulation risk both argue against chronic high-dose isolated supplementation beyond what food and modest fortification already provide.

## Risks

**Excess intake:** High BCAA intake — including isoleucine — can elevate ammonia and interfere with serotonin signaling, producing brain fog and cognitive dulling. Animal longevity data suggest excess isoleucine actively counters healthspan benefits, making "more is better" thinking inappropriate here.

**Metabolic toxicity pathway:** Isoleucine catabolism generates propionyl-CoA, which requires biotin-dependent propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC) to clear. If PCC is impaired — by biotin deficiency or genetic mutation (propionic acidemia) — propionyl-CoA, propionic acid, and methylcitrate accumulate, causing metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neurotoxicity. Higher isoleucine intake amplifies this risk.

**No established Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL)** has been formally set, but the risk signals above — particularly at chronically elevated intakes on already high-protein diets — are clinically meaningful.

**Nutrient interactions:**
- **Thiamine (B1):** The branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase complex (BCKDHC) is TPP-dependent; thiamine deficiency impairs isoleucine catabolism and promotes toxic intermediate buildup.
- **Biotin:** Required by PCC downstream; high isoleucine or high total protein intake increases biotin demand proportionally.
- **Serotonin:** High-dose BCAAs compete with tryptophan for brain uptake, suppressing serotonin synthesis — a concern with chronic high-dose isolated supplementation.

Anyone with maple syrup urine disease or propionic acidemia should manage isoleucine intake only under clinical supervision.
