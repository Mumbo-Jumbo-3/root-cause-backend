# Arginine

Aliases: L-arginine

## What it's for

Arginine is a conditionally essential amino acid — the body synthesizes it endogenously but demand can outpace supply under stress, trauma, or high protein intake. Its most central role is serving as the substrate for nitric oxide synthase (NOS), producing nitric oxide (NO), the primary vasodilator in blood vessels. NO regulates blood pressure, arterial smooth muscle relaxation, platelet aggregation, neurotransmission, and neuroendocrine secretion. Beyond vascular health, arginine drives the urea cycle to clear ammonia, contributes to creatine synthesis (alongside glycine and methionine), supports polyamine production, and modulates neurotransmitters including glutamate and dopamine. It also plays a recognized role in wound healing and immune function, particularly in trauma settings. Deficiency is associated with poor wound healing, hair loss, impaired ammonia clearance, and compromised endothelial function.

## How to get it

**Food sources**

Arginine is abundant in protein-rich foods:

- **Animal sources:** Turkey breast, chicken, pork loin, beef, salmon, eggs, dairy, bone broth/gelatin (gelatin is notably high in arginine, glycine, and hydroxyproline)
- **Plant sources:** Pumpkin seeds, soybeans, peanuts, spirulina, garlic

Whole-food sources come packaged with cofactors and minerals that modulate arginine metabolism, making them the preferred baseline approach over isolated supplements for most people.

**Supplement forms**

- **Free-form L-arginine:** Most common, well-absorbed, standard form used in clinical research
- **Arginine HCl:** Highly soluble and stable; used in clinical and intravenous preparations
- **Arginine alpha-ketoglutarate (AAKG):** Popular in pre-workout blends; theoretical metabolic advantages, though bioavailability benefits over free-form remain debated
- **L-citrulline (indirect precursor):** Generally superior for raising plasma arginine levels because it bypasses first-pass intestinal metabolism; functionally interchangeable for NO and urea cycle support, and often preferable for those sensitive to direct arginine supplementation

Combining L-arginine with beetroot juice (which supplies dietary nitrates via an alternative NO-production pathway) produces an additive effect on nitric oxide output.

## Dosing

There is no established RDA for arginine in healthy adults. Supplemental use is tiered by goal:

- **Cardiovascular and endothelial support:** ~3 g/day, the minimum dose consistently used in clinical trials for blood pressure and vascular function
- **Performance and muscle pump:** 3–6 g taken 30–60 minutes before exercise to maximize NO-mediated vasodilation during training
- **Urea cycle / high-protein diet support:** 2–6 g/day as needed
- **Clinical repletion (trauma, immune support):** Up to 10–30 g/day under medical supervision only — not a self-directed protocol

For cardiovascular or metabolic goals, splitting the daily dose across two administrations helps maintain steadier plasma levels given arginine's relatively short half-life. Pre-workout timing is most appropriate when the target is performance. If GI tolerance is a concern at higher doses, L-citrulline at 3–6 g may deliver equivalent or better plasma arginine elevation with less digestive upset.

## Risks

At 3–6 g/day, L-arginine is generally well-tolerated in healthy adults. The most common side effects are gastrointestinal — bloating, nausea, and diarrhea — particularly at higher doses.

**Nitric oxide overproduction:** Excess arginine can paradoxically worsen vascular outcomes. NO reacts with superoxide anion to form peroxynitrite, a cytotoxic compound that damages biological targets and can shut off mitochondrial energy production. This superoxide/peroxynitrite imbalance is implicated in hypertension development.

**Herpes reactivation:** Herpes simplex virus depends on arginine for replication. High-dose supplementation can trigger outbreaks — individuals with herpes should avoid arginine supplements.

**Autoimmune conditions:** Arginine and citrulline may provoke flares or increased inflammation in people with autoimmune diseases.

**Drug interactions:** Arginine significantly lowers blood pressure via NO pathways. Combining it with PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil/Viagra, tadalafil) or nitrate medications can cause dangerous hypotension — this combination should be avoided. Amino acid imbalances from megadosing (e.g., alongside excessive glutamate, aspartate, or cysteine) carry additional excitotoxicity concerns.

**Urea cycle disorders:** Individuals with relevant genetic sensitivities risk hyperammonemia from arginine loading.

**Cancer biology:** Animal data suggest that the balance between arginine's NOS and polyamine pathways has meaningful implications for colon cancer risk; high polyamine activity is associated with tumor promotion.
