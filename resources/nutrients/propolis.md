# Propolis

Aliases: bee propolis, bee glue

## What it's for

Propolis is a resin-like substance produced by bees to protect the hive, and its broad biological activity in humans reflects that same protective role. Its most distinctive compound is caffeic acid phenethyl ester (CAPE) — found in no other known source — which supports aromatase inhibition, reduces inflammation, protects the liver and cardiovascular system, and has demonstrated anti-cancer and neuroprotective properties. Beyond CAPE, propolis is rich in polyphenols and flavonoids that deliver antimicrobial, antifungal (including anti-candida), and antioxidant effects. It supports androgenic hormone pathways by promoting LH, FSH, and testosterone while opposing serotonin and inhibiting 5-lipoxygenase. It also boosts BDNF for brain health, strengthens gut tight junctions, improves microbiome diversity, and in one clinical trial raised pregnancy rates to 60% (versus 20% for placebo) in women with endometriosis-linked infertility over nine months.

## How to get it

There is no significant dietary food source of propolis beyond small amounts present in some raw honeys. Practical acquisition comes through deliberate use across several forms:

- **Liquid extract (standardized):** Alcohol-free glycerin-based drops offer strong bioavailability for propolis's lipophilic constituents like CAPE; ideal for immune, respiratory, and oral applications.
- **Capsules/softgels:** Convenient for daily standardized dosing; fat-soluble delivery (softgels with oil) improves absorption of lipophilic compounds.
- **Red propolis softgels:** Derived from mangrove sources, this form has a distinct isoflavonoid profile not found in standard brown or yellow propolis — a meaningful phytochemical difference, not just a color variation.
- **Chewable raw propolis:** Provides direct oral and throat contact, supporting oral microbiome health and candida management; can also be incorporated into oral hygiene routines alongside propolis-containing toothpaste.
- **Propolis-infused raw honey (homemade):** A practical whole-food-adjacent option combining benefits of both substances; use unheated honey to preserve active compounds.
- **Topical use:** Applied directly to wounds, burns, or the scalp for localized antimicrobial and hair-loss-protective effects.

**GI sensitivity note:** Some individuals find oral propolis too harsh on the stomach. Royal jelly is a gentler alternative delivering most overlapping benefits — with the exception of antifungal activity, where propolis remains superior and cannot be substituted.

## Dosing

No official RDI or RDA exists for propolis; dosing guidance is based on clinical trials and practitioner consensus. A standard daily range of **300–600 mg** is appropriate for general health maintenance, taken as 300 mg twice daily or 600 mg as a single dose. When taken once daily at 600 mg, afternoon or evening timing is preferred, and all oral doses should be taken with a fat-containing meal to support absorption of lipophilic constituents. For higher-demand therapeutic contexts — such as the endometriosis-fertility protocol — **500 mg twice daily (1,000 mg/day)** was used safely over nine months under clinical observation. A cycling protocol of **4–6 weeks on, 1–2 weeks off** is recommended regardless of dose to prevent tolerance and preserve sensitivity to active compounds. Do not extrapolate the 1,000 mg/day dose to self-directed use without medical supervision.

## Risks

**GI side effects** — nausea, stomach upset, and diarrhea — are the most common complaints, particularly at higher doses or in sensitive individuals. Switching to a softgel or liquid form, or taking with food, can mitigate this; persistent intolerance warrants switching to royal jelly as a substitute.

**Allergic reactions** are a meaningful risk: individuals with bee allergies, hay fever, or sensitivity to bee products (beeswax, honey) face a genuine cross-reactivity risk and should not use propolis without medical guidance.

**No formal Tolerable Upper Limit (UL)** has been established by any regulatory body. The highest dose in documented human trials is 1,000 mg/day over nine months, which was well-tolerated in that study population — but this cannot be taken as a universal safety ceiling.

**Long-term safety data are limited**, reinforcing the case for cycling rather than indefinite continuous use.

**Drug interactions to flag:**
- **Anticoagulants (e.g., warfarin):** Propolis may potentiate blood-thinning effects; concurrent use requires medical oversight and possible INR monitoring.
- **Diabetes medications:** Propolis may have additive blood-glucose-lowering effects, raising hypoglycemia risk in those on insulin or oral hypoglycemics.

Consult a clinician before using propolis alongside any of these medications.
