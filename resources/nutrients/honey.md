# Honey

Aliases: raw honey, manuka honey

## What it's for

Honey is one of the oldest therapeutic foods in medicine, valued for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties. Its unique sugar composition — free fructose and free glucose alongside hundreds of bioactive phenolic compounds — supports more efficient cellular energy metabolism than refined sugar does, rapidly replenishing liver glycogen while lowering C-reactive protein, homocysteine, triglycerides, and blood glucose. Raw honey protects against obesity and insulin resistance, improves allergy and respiratory symptoms by inhibiting mast cell histamine release and IgE responses, and accelerates wound healing by eradicating bacterial infections and optimizing collagen synthesis. Specialized varieties such as Manuka honey disrupt bacterial biofilms — including *Staphylococcus aureus*, *E. coli*, and *Candida albicans* — through methylglyoxal and osmotic mechanisms, making honey therapeutically relevant far beyond simple sweetening.

## How to get it

**Raw vs. processed:** Raw, unheated honey is meaningfully superior to pasteurized or processed forms. Heating destroys active enzymes and reduces phenolic content, degrading both its nutritional and therapeutic value. Always choose honey labeled raw, unfiltered, and unpasteurized.

**Varieties and their strengths:**
- **High-phenolic raw wildflower honey** (e.g., Irish heather, ~68 mg GAE/100g) — best for general antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic support
- **Manuka honey** (rated by UMF or MGO) — highest antimicrobial and antibiofilm potency; suitable for internal and topical use
- **Buckwheat honey** — notably high in antioxidants; a good general-purpose raw option

**Complementary hive products to pair with honey:**
- **Bee pollen** (glyphosate-free) — adds silica, calcium, zinc, selenium, magnesium, and flavonoids; supports allergy relief and vascular health
- **Propolis-infused honey** — antifungal, antimicrobial, rich in polyphenols; beneficial for lungs, prostate, and candida management; also applicable topically
- **Royal jelly** — paired with honey, adds androgen- and fertility-supporting properties

**Trace minerals:** Just four tablespoons of raw honey can meet daily requirements for several trace minerals including potassium and boron, making sourcing quality critical — choose honey from low-pesticide, low-pollution environments.

## Dosing

Honey has no official RDI or % DV, as it is a food rather than a classified essential nutrient. Use the following clinical and practitioner benchmarks:

- **General daily maintenance:** 1–3 teaspoons (10–17g) supports liver glycogen, enzyme intake, and trace mineral needs
- **Allergy or cough relief:** 3–5 teaspoons (15–25g) daily for allergies; 10g (roughly 2 teaspoons) before bed for persistent cough
- **Anti-inflammatory / metabolic repletion:** 75g daily for two weeks has produced measurable reductions in blood sugar, CRP, homocysteine, and triglycerides in clinical studies
- **Tissue-protective protocol:** 1.2 g/kg body weight (≈90g for a 75 kg adult) has been used to reduce markers of tissue damage and raise antioxidant activity

**Timing:** Pre- or post-exercise is a practical window given honey's rapid liver glycogen replenishment. Evening dosing (≈10g before bed) is specifically evidenced for cough suppression. Higher therapeutic doses (75–90g/day) are short-term repletion protocols, not indefinite maintenance targets.

## Risks

**Blood sugar and fructose load:** Honey contains roughly 40% fructose. Moderate doses appear to lower blood glucose and CRP in research, but chronic high intake may raise HbA1c, contribute to hypertriglyceridemia or uric acid elevation, and cause blood sugar spikes — particularly in diabetics or individuals with hypertension. Therapeutic benefit appears dose-dependent; metabolically compromised individuals should use caution and seek clinical guidance.

**Infants:** No honey — raw or processed — should be given to infants under 12 months due to risk of *Clostridium botulinum* spore contamination.

**Sourcing toxin burden:** Honey from pesticide- or glyphosate-exposed hives may carry environmental toxins. Prioritize independently lab-tested, single-source products from low-pollution areas.

**Topical/biofilm use:** When using Manuka honey as a mucosal biofilm disruptor, support the mucosal layer for four weeks before beginning, as premature use may be harsh on unprepared tissue.

**No established upper limit:** No official Tolerable Upper Intake Level exists. Above ~90g/day, gut fermentation issues, uric acid elevation, or triglyceride increases become plausible in sensitive individuals.

**Drug interactions:** No specific interactions are documented in the available evidence, but honey's effects on blood glucose, insulin, CRP, and homocysteine mean concurrent use with antidiabetic medications (metformin, insulin) or anticoagulants warrants clinical monitoring.
