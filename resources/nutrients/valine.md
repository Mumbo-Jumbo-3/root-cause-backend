# Valine

Aliases: L-valine

## What it's for

Valine is an essential branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) the body cannot make on its own, so it must come entirely from food or supplements. It plays a central role in energy metabolism, feeding breakdown products into the Krebs cycle via the BCKDHC enzyme complex. Valine is also critical for hematopoietic stem cell function — supporting blood cell production — and for muscle coordination and neurological health. At the blood-brain barrier, valine competes with tryptophan for transport into the brain, meaning valine levels in the bloodstream actively influence serotonin synthesis. Structurally, valine belongs to the same saturated carbon-chain family as compounds with known GABAergic activity, suggesting a possible role in inhibitory neurotransmitter pathways, though this remains less established in humans.

## How to get it

**Whole food sources are the preferred route** because food-bound valine arrives with co-factors — biotin, thiamine, and others — that support its own metabolism.

- **Animal proteins:** Grass-finished beef (including cuts like skirt steak), bone marrow, and grass-fed gelatin are prioritized over isolated amino acid supplements by nutrition researchers focused on whole-food synergy.
- **Plant-based / alternative sources:** AFA algae (*Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*) provides all nine essential amino acids, including valine, within a ~60–70% protein matrix alongside bioactive compounds like phenylethylamine.

**Supplement forms:**
- **Free-form L-valine powder** — absorbed rapidly without requiring digestion; useful when food intake is inadequate or demand is elevated.
- **Combined BCAA formulas (leucine : isoleucine : valine, often 2:1:1)** — preferred over isolated valine for exercise and recovery contexts, as the three BCAAs work synergistically.

**Bioavailability note:** High valine intake — whether from food or supplements — increases demand for both **biotin** (required by propionyl-CoA carboxylase in valine's breakdown pathway) and **thiamine/B1** (required by the BCKDHC enzyme complex). Ensure these cofactors are adequate, especially on high-protein diets.

## Dosing

The WHO/FAO estimated average requirement for valine is approximately **26 mg/kg/day**, translating to roughly **1,820 mg/day** for a 70 kg adult under normal maintenance conditions. Most people eating adequate protein from whole foods meet this threshold without supplementation.

For **higher-demand states** — intense training, illness, or active blood cell turnover support — combined BCAA supplements are more useful than isolated valine. Typical total BCAA doses range from **5–20 g/day**, with valine comprising roughly one-quarter of that total in a standard 2:1:1 leucine:isoleucine:valine ratio (approximately 1.25–5 g valine/day from the blend).

**Timing:** Distribute valine intake across meals or consume around training sessions. Note that taking valine alongside carbohydrates triggers an insulin response that clears competing amino acids — including valine itself — from the bloodstream, which may increase tryptophan uptake into the brain and support serotonin synthesis. Those trying to minimize serotonin suppression should be mindful of this interaction.

## Risks

No formal Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) has been established for valine specifically, but chronic high-dose isolated valine or BCAA supplementation carries real risks worth understanding.

**Neurological and metabolic effects:** Excess valine raises blood ammonia levels and suppresses tryptophan entry into the brain — both mechanisms associated with brain fog, cognitive dullness, and potentially reduced serotonin output. This is especially relevant for people already prone to low mood or serotonin dysregulation.

**Nutrient interactions:**
- **Thiamine (B1):** Valine catabolism depends on the TPP-dependent BCKDHC enzyme. Thiamine deficiency impairs this step, causing toxic accumulation of branched-chain ketoacids. High valine intake without adequate B1 is risky.
- **Biotin:** The downstream metabolite propionyl-CoA requires biotin-dependent carboxylase to proceed. High-protein or high-valine diets increase biotin demand; deficiency can bottleneck valine metabolism.
- **Tryptophan:** Valine directly competes with tryptophan at shared blood-brain barrier transporters. Very high valine intake can meaningfully suppress serotonin synthesis.

**Drug interactions:** No formally documented drug interactions exist for valine, but individuals taking **SSRIs or other serotonergic medications** should exercise caution with high BCAA intake — by limiting tryptophan's brain access, large valine doses could theoretically blunt the efficacy of serotonergic drugs. Consult a clinician before combining high-dose BCAAs with any serotonin-related medication.
