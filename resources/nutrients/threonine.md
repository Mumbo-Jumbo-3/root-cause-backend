# Threonine

Aliases: L-threonine

## What it's for

Threonine is an essential amino acid the body cannot produce, making dietary or supplemental intake necessary. It plays a central role in gut health by producing the mucus gel layer that protects the intestinal lining and supports immune defense at the gut barrier. It also aids T-cell production in the thymus, contributes to muscle contraction, helps prevent fat accumulation in the liver, and supports skin and tooth integrity. Metabolically, threonine is catabolized into succinyl-CoA for entry into the Krebs cycle, making it a meaningful contributor to cellular energy production. This catabolism depends on biotin as a cofactor, meaning threonine function is only as good as your cofactor status allows.

## How to get it

**Food sources** provide threonine in its most bioavailable form as part of complete proteins:
- Beef, raw milk, and pasture-raised eggs are top animal-based sources
- Chicken is another reliable source and pairs well with lysine supplementation to round out incomplete protein combinations
- Plant-based sources such as AFA algae contain threonine but offer generally lower bioavailability than animal proteins

**Supplement forms:**
- **Free-form L-threonine powder** — highest purity, most dosing flexibility, no excipients; ideal for precise intake
- **L-threonine capsules** — convenient; quality varies significantly by excipient profile (look for HPMC-only capsules, no magnesium stearate, titanium dioxide, or carrageenan)
- **Essential amino acid (EAA) blends** — deliver all nine EAAs including threonine in balanced ratios; practical for athletes with broad amino acid needs rather than targeted threonine support

## Dosing

The WHO/FAO baseline requirement for adults is approximately **15 mg/kg/day** (~1,050 mg for a 70 kg adult), with the RDA slightly higher at roughly **20 mg/kg/day** (~1,400 mg/day). Most people with varied diets that include animal protein meet this through food alone.

For targeted supplementation — gut lining support, immune function, or liver protection — the commonly used range is **1,000–5,000 mg/day** of L-threonine. Athletes, individuals on high-protein diets, or those with compromised gut integrity fall in the higher end of this range due to increased amino acid catabolism. When threonine is delivered via an EAA blend, total serving sizes are typically **6–15 g of combined EAAs**, with threonine as one component.

No formal timing protocol exists specifically for threonine, but splitting doses across meals rather than taking large single doses aligns with general amino acid absorption principles and reduces metabolic burden.

## Risks

Threonine has a favorable safety profile and no established Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL), as adverse effects from dietary intake are not well-documented in healthy adults. However, at high supplemental doses, impaired catabolism can cause buildup of propionyl-CoA, propionic acid, and methylcitrate, leading to metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neurotoxicity — risks that rise sharply if cofactor status is inadequate.

**Biotin interaction:** Biotin is a required coenzyme for threonine catabolism via propionyl-CoA carboxylase. Biotin deficiency impairs this pathway, blocking gluconeogenesis and energy production. Anyone supplementing threonine at higher doses should ensure sufficient biotin intake. Note that biotin supplementation can skew lab results — a one-week washout before blood work is advisable.

**Vitamin B12 interaction:** B12 supports the downstream conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA; deficiency compounds the same toxic intermediate buildup risk.

**High-protein diet interaction:** Very high protein intakes increase threonine catabolism, paradoxically raising the risk of byproduct accumulation if biotin and B12 are not maintained.

Individuals with pre-existing kidney or liver conditions should use caution, as these organs handle amino acid catabolism and nitrogen excretion. Chronic high-dose single amino acid supplementation without balancing overall amino acid intake can also disturb nitrogen metabolism.
